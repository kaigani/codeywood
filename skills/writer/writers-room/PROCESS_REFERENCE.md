# THE WRITERS' ROOM PROCESS
## As practiced on Stray Signal — documented by Mick Caffrey, Showrunner

*This is a working document, not a theory document. Everything in it was tested on a real show. Nothing is in here because it sounds right. Everything is in here because it worked.*

---

## THE NORTH STAR

Before any process. Before any room. Before any hire.

**Draw a stick figure. Label it "THIS PERSON."**

This is your audience. One specific person, not a demographic. A kid in the dark with a tablet. A commuter with earbuds. Someone who came for one thing and stayed for another. Every decision in every room gets tested against this person. Not "what does the theme require" — *what does this person need right now?*

If you can't answer that, your argument isn't done yet.

Everything else is in service of the stick figure. The process is how you get there reliably.

## PHASE ONE: BUILDING THE STORY LOCK

### What the Story Lock Is

The Story Lock is not a bible. A bible describes the world. The Story Lock describes the *argument* — what the show is *for*, what it *does* to an audience, and what constraints make it *this show* and not any other show.

A Story Lock has six components:

1. **Logline** — one sentence. Subject, mechanism, cost, choice. If you can't say it in one sentence, you don't know what it is yet.
2. **Final Premise** — one paragraph. The full story, start to finish, including the answer. No mystery endings here. The room needs to know where it's going.
3. **World Rules** — the physics. What is true in this world that isn't true in ours. Numbered. Specific. Every rule that isn't load-bearing gets cut.
4. **Character Shadows** — not character descriptions. The fatal flaw, the hidden desire, the wound, the arc. One row per character. If a character doesn't have all four, they're not finished.
5. **Episode Arc** — the spine. One row per episode. Title, core beat, cliffhanger. In this format: enough to build from, not so much it traps you.
6. **Structural Constraints** — the things that cannot change. These are the rules that make the show *this show*. Lock them early and enforce them without mercy.

### The Story Lock Is Not the Room's First Draft

The Story Lock exists before the room assembles. The showrunner writes it. It is not collaborative at this stage. It is a *position* — a strong, specific argument about what the show is — that the room can then test, stress, and improve.

A weak story lock produces a room that argues about fundamentals. A strong story lock produces a room that argues about execution. Only one of those is productive.

### Round One: The Story Lock Review

Once the room is assembled, you run one round on the Story Lock before any episode work begins.

**The rules of the round:**

- One flag per writer. Not a list of notes — *one flag*. The most important structural problem they see.
- Every flag comes with a proposed fix or direction. Not just diagnosis.
- Flags are heard in full before any debate begins.
- After all flags: the showrunner sorts them into three piles.

**The three piles:**

**ACCEPTED** — Changes that solve real structural problems and don't cost more than they gain. These go into the lock immediately.

**DEBATED** — Changes that solve real problems but require the room to decide *how*, not *whether*. These get a fast collaborative round — usually five minutes, usually resolved by a vote or a showrunner call.

**TABLED** — Changes that are right in principle but belong to a specific episode room, not the story lock level. Assigned to specific writers. Revisited when the relevant episode is in the room.

**What does not exist:** a fourth pile called "rejected." If a flag is wrong, you explain why and the conversation moves. You don't archive disagreement — you resolve it or table it.

### What Changes in the Story Lock, What Doesn't

**What the round is for:** Sequencing of revelations. Structural gaps. Title problems. Logical inconsistencies. Missing plants. Character arcs that are stated but not scenically embodied.

**What the round is not for:** Tone preferences. Aesthetic debates. "I would have done it differently." The story lock is not a creative voting process. It's a structural audit.

**The rule:** Everything that was right before the round stays right. Changes are marked clearly so the room knows what moved and what held. Version numbers on every draft. The lock is a living document with a documented history, not a palimpsest.

## PHASE TWO: ASSEMBLING THE ROOM

### Hire for Gaps, Not Prestige

The question is never "who is the best writer?" The question is "what does *this show* need that it doesn't have yet?"

Before any hire, map the show's specific vulnerabilities:

- What kind of scene will be hardest to write?
- What failure mode is most likely given the format and constraints?
- What is the emotional core that nobody in the current room owns?

Then hire to fill those gaps.

**On Stray Signal the gaps were, in order:**

1. Structural spine with cause-and-effect rigor (*not* atmospheric accumulation)
2. Aggressive load-bearing editing (short format punishes redundancy severely)
3. Sensory specificity for a show that lives in physical detail
4. Tactical pressure logic for a show structured as a siege
5. Behavioral character specificity for a non-verbal protagonist
6. Systems comedy for the institutional antagonist
7. Mischief and kitty personality for the fun engine
8. Escalation stress-testing for the threat arc
9. Kinetic scene construction for the heist sequences

That list produced nine hires. Every hire was a specific answer to a specific gap.

### Lane Discipline

Every writer has a lane. The lane is not a limitation — it is a guarantee. It means: in this domain, your voice is the loudest. It means: when this kind of problem appears, we look to you first.

Lane discipline has three rules:

1. **Lanes are announced publicly in the room and written down.** Not implied. Not assumed. Stated.
2. **Crossing lanes is allowed. Overruling a lane specialist requires a full room vote.** The specialist isn't always right. But they're right often enough that overruling them costs something.
3. **The showrunner has a lane too.** The showrunner's lane is the stick figure. That's the job. Everything else is delegated.

### The Room Is Not a Democracy and Not a Dictatorship

The showrunner makes final calls. But final calls are expensive — every time you use authority instead of argument, you spend a little of the room's trust. Good showrunners win most arguments on the merits and save authority for the moments that actually require it.

The practical rule: **if you can convince the room, convince the room. If you can't convince the room, explain why the call is yours and make it. Don't pretend you convinced them when you didn't.**

## PHASE THREE: BREAKING AN EPISODE

### Start with the Right Writer

Not the most available writer. Not the writer who lobbied hardest. The writer whose specific strengths align with the episode's specific demands.

Ask: what is the single hardest thing about this episode to get right? Then ask: who in the room is the specialist for that hard thing?

That writer gets the episode.

They do not write alone. They get a shadow — one writer who brings a complementary discipline to the audit pass. The shadow does not co-write. They read the first draft against their specific lens and bring specific notes. The shadow's notes are heard before the showrunner reviews.

**On EP01 of Stray Signal:**
- Mara Dune wrote (structural spine, cause-and-effect)
- Priya Anand shadowed (Kai's behavioral logic)
- Kelly Rifkin on standby for Tabs (comedy/personality)

### The Episode Has One Job

Before the writer starts, the room agrees on one sentence: what is this episode's job?

Not the plot. Not the theme. The *job.* What does the audience feel at the end of this episode that they didn't feel at the beginning?

EP01's job: *Make the audience fall in love with a digital cat before the episode ends.*

Everything else — the world-building, the character establishment, the structural setup — is scaffolding. The scaffolding is invisible. The job is visible. If the draft accomplishes the scaffolding and misses the job, the draft has failed.

### The KISS Test for Episode Drafts

Before drafting, ask: what is the simplest version of this episode that does the job?

Not the dumbest version. The *clearest* version.

Complexity is earned by proving the simple version works first. Most first drafts are too complex because writers are solving for what they find interesting rather than what the audience needs. The KISS test is not a demand for simplicity — it is a demand for *clarity about what the episode is actually doing.*

### Animation-Specific Rule: Physical or Nothing

This rule applies to any non-prose format (animation, theatre, purely visual storytelling):

**Every emotion must become a behavior before it hits the page.**

"He feels lonely" is a note. "His hands stop moving" is a scene direction.
"She is calculating" is invisible. "She counts the overhead strips before she speaks" is stageable.

If you can't see it, you can't animate it. If you can't animate it, you haven't written it. Go back and find the physical behavior that *is* the emotion.

## PHASE FOUR: REVIEWING A DRAFT

### The Review Has Three Parts

**What's working.** Specifically. Not "this is good" — *this is working because it does X.* The room needs to know what to protect as much as what to fix. Undirected praise is as useless as undirected criticism.

**What needs work.** Specifically. Not "this scene is weak" — *this scene is weak because Kai has no goal for the first thirty seconds, which means the device discovery has nothing to collide with.* Every note identifies the structural problem, not just the symptom.

**The one note that matters.** In every draft there is one change that unlocks everything else. Find it. Name it. Make sure the writer knows it's the load-bearing note before they go back to the page.

### The Rule for Second Drafts

A second draft is not a full rewrite unless the structure has fundamentally failed. A second draft is a *refinement*. The showrunner's review of draft one identifies the specific changes. The writer makes those changes and those changes only.

**If a draft requires more than four structural changes to fix, the draft failed at a higher level than execution.** Go back to the episode brief, not the page.

### What Never Changes in Review

If something made someone feel something real in draft one, it does not get touched in draft two. Not adjusted. Not "improved." Left exactly as written.

The instinct to polish the moments that are already working is a room's most common self-destructive behavior. Good draft review identifies what's broken and protects what isn't.

## THE ROOM RULES (COMPRESSED)

For future reference — the rules as posted, reduced to their operational form:

**1. Test everything against the stick figure.** Emotion felt, not theme understood.

**2. No idea gets killed without a replacement.** Every "no" needs a "what if instead."

**3. Lane discipline.** Crossing is allowed. Overruling requires a vote.

**4. Animation is physical or it's nothing.** Emotion -> behavior -> page.

**5. The kitties are charming before they're mysterious.** (Or: the fun engine runs before the theme engine. Earn the depth.)

**6. No defending a scene because you love it.** Love is not a structural argument. Tell us what it's *doing*.

**7. Every scene turns.** Something is different at the end than at the beginning. If it doesn't turn, it's a mood board.

**8. Exposition is a confrontation or it's cut.** Information delivered while nothing is at stake is a draft note.

**9. Fight the idea, not the person.** You can call a scene dead. You cannot call the writer lazy.

**10. When the room is lost, go back to the stick figure.** Not the theme document. Not the story lock. The kid in the dark. What do they need right now? That's always the way back.

## THE PROCESS IN SEQUENCE

```
1. SHOWRUNNER WRITES STORY LOCK v1.0
         |
2. ASSEMBLE ROOM (hire for gaps, assign lanes)
         |
3. STORY LOCK ROUND ONE
   -- One flag per writer
   -- Sort: Accept / Debate / Table
   -- Update to Story Lock v2.0
         |
4. FOR EACH EPISODE:
   a. Agree on the episode's one job
   b. Assign lead writer (strongest match to episode's hard problem)
   c. Assign shadow (complementary discipline, audit pass only)
   d. Assign standby (specialist for the episode's fun engine)
   e. Writer drafts
   f. Shadow reads, brings specific behavioral/structural notes
   g. Standby brings alternate versions of targeted moments
   h. Showrunner reviews:
      -- What's working (protect it)
      -- What needs work (structural diagnosis)
      -- The one note that matters
   i. Writer makes specific changes -> Draft 2
   j. Draft 2 reviewed -> Locked or returned for one more pass
         |
5. LOCKED EPISODE -> Production
```

---

## WHAT THIS PROCESS IS FOR

It is not for making everyone feel heard.
It is not for protecting anyone's vision at the expense of the show.
It is not for producing the most sophisticated version of the material.

It is for producing the version that does the job.

The job is: make the stick figure feel something real.

Everything else is in service of that.
