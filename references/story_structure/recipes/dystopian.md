# Dystopian (Oppression + Awakening + Revolt)

## Slot
Serialized fiction where a citizen-protagonist living inside an explicitly engineered society (totalitarian state, walled compound, controlled feed, segregated tier, post-collapse regime) is shown the seam between the official story and the actual mechanism, undergoes a moral awakening, and either ignites or attempts revolt against the System. The genre's argument is political: the regime is illegitimate, and the protagonist's awakening is the audience's permission to see their own world differently.

## Corpus
- *The Hunger Games* (Collins)
- *1984* (Orwell)
- *The Handmaid's Tale* (Atwood / Hulu)
- *Divergent* (Roth)
- *The Maze Runner* (Dashner)
- *V for Vendetta* (Moore / Wachowskis)
- *Children of Men* (Cuarón)
- *Snowpiercer* (Bong / TNT)
- *Equilibrium* (Wimmer)
- *Silo* (AppleTV+ / Howey)
- *Fahrenheit 451* (Bradbury)
- *A Clockwork Orange* (Burgess / Kubrick)
- *Brave New World* (Huxley)
- *Black Mirror* — "Fifteen Million Merits"
- *Logan's Run* (Nolan / Johnson)

(Tonal range: from YA-Hunger-Games kinetic and Maze Runner / Divergent's puzzle-action through Snowpiercer's pulp-class-violence and V for Vendetta's stylized polemic to literary-1984's prestige-grim and Atwood / Cuarón's prestige-political-elegiac. Same recipe — citizen sees the seam, wakes up, moves.)

## Emotional contract
**Oppression + awakening + revolt.** Audience opens compressed inside the regime's logic — uniforms, rules, broadcasts, rituals — and breathes shallow. The awakening is the genre's drug: the moment the protagonist sees the official story as story. Revolt is the cocktail's release — not necessarily victory, sometimes the act of refusal alone. The cocktail is political pressure relieved into action.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Regime's Logic** — A repeated visual and procedural language — uniforms, ration cards, broadcasts, rituals, surveillance equipment, anthems, banned-words list, prayer routines, the daily ceremony. Worldbuilding through repetition, not exposition.
- **The First Crack** — A moment in the pilot or Episode 2 when the official story misaligns with what the protagonist sees with their own eyes. A neighbor disappears. A broadcast contradicts a memory. A book is too heavy. This is the seed.
- **The Awakening Beat** — The midpoint moment when the protagonist sees the regime as a constructed thing — usually catalyzed by the Disruptor's act, death, or revelation. Obedience ends here.
- **The Symbol Made Public** — Late-stage, the protagonist or movement makes a SIGN — a salute, a defaced poster, a banned name spoken aloud, fire on the broadcast tower, a single raised hand. The genre's release.
- **The Cost of Awakening** — The Disruptor, the Memory, or the Tether pays. The recipe charges admission for sight.

**Supporting tropes:**
- **The Daily Ceremony** — A repeated ritual the regime requires — Two Minutes Hate, anthem, Tessellation, ranking, the Wall update, prayer. The audience learns the regime through its rituals.
- **The Banned Object** — Book, photograph, song, food, name, letter — a small contraband item that becomes the Awakening's lever.
- **The Selection** — A ceremony at which the regime CHOOSES bodies for use — Reaping, Choosing, Ceremony, the Wall, the lottery, the Initiation. Often the inciting moment.
- **The Loyalist's Speech** — Mid-season, the Loyalist (peer) delivers an articulate defense of the regime's logic to the protagonist. The genre is honest enough to give the regime its best argument.
- **The Broadcast Truth** — A late-stage moment where the regime's machinery (TV, feed, anthem, ceremony, prayer line) is hijacked, defaced, or replaced. The Symbol Made Public's vehicle.
- **The Defector Pipeline** — Underground route — train, tunnel, wall section, dead-drop, code phrase, sympathetic guard — that smuggles bodies / messages / books past the regime. Usually compromised by midpoint.
- **The Interrogation Scene** — Regime's Face or proxy questions the protagonist; the scene's tension is whether they break, lie, or pretend. Often the climax's gate.
- **The Wall Test** — A physical demonstration of the regime's geographic claim — wall, fence, district line, sector boundary, train order, tier divide. The protagonist crosses or witnesses a crossing.
- **The Loved One Used Against** — Regime threatens or holds the Tether to force the protagonist's obedience. The personal made into a lever.
- **The Underground Cell Meeting** — Resistance gathers in a basement / forest / kitchen / chapel and talks doctrine, plans, names. Genre's quiet beat.
- **The Banned Book Read Aloud** — A scene where forbidden text is heard for the first time — poetry, scripture, history, name list. The Awakening's instrument.
- **The Public Punishment** — Regime executes / disappears / shames a body in front of the citizenry; the protagonist witnesses. The Cost of Awakening made public.
- **The Doubled Mirror** — The Regime's Face and the protagonist are shown to share a quality — class, background, intelligence, ideology — and the genre asks the audience to feel that proximity.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't have the protagonist already be a rebel at episode 1. The Obedient Citizen who wakes up is the engine. Born-revolutionaries belong to action-pulp, not dystopian.
- Don't make the Regime cartoonish. The Regime's Face must articulate the ideology in a way that, briefly, makes sense. The audience must understand why people obey.
- Don't let the awakening be cost-free. The Disruptor, the Memory, or the Tether pays. Cheap awakenings read as power fantasy, not dystopian.
- Don't resolve in clean total victory. Even YA-register dystopian (Hunger Games) ends with the protagonist scarred and the politics unfinished. Total clean revolution belongs to fantasy / war.
- Don't omit the Daily Ceremony. The regime's logic must be lived through, not described. If the audience never sees a broadcast / ration / anthem / prayer, the worldbuilding is exposition.
- Don't reduce the protagonist's awakening to a single info-drop. The cracks accumulate; the seeing is gradual; the action is late. Speed-running it produces YA-action, not dystopian.
- Don't have the Memory character get out alive AND the regime fall. The genre needs a witness who paid for the truth they passed on.

## Conformity rules
- The protagonist must start OBEDIENT. The genre is the awakening of someone inside the regime, not the arrival of a rebel from outside. Outside-rebel protagonists belong to war / pulp.
- The Regime's Logic must be SHOWN through ritual, broadcast, ceremony, uniform, ration. Worldbuilding by repetition, not exposition.
- The Regime's Face must articulate the ideology with apparent competence. If the antagonist is stupid, the genre's argument collapses.
- The awakening must be GRADUAL — cracks accumulate across episodes, not delivered in one info-drop. Speed-run awakenings read as YA action.
- The Cost of Awakening must be paid by the Disruptor, the Memory, or the Tether. Cheap awakenings read as power fantasy.
- The climax must enact the REVOLT — a defacement, a hijack, a refusal, a flight, a public sign. Even if the regime survives. The audience must see the gesture.
- The Memory / Old Hand must give and pay. Knowledge cannot survive unscathed.

- Common failure mode: dystopian as action-flavor. If the regime is just a costume on a war story, the genre collapses to YA action-adventure. The Regime's Logic must be specific, lived, and articulated.
- Common failure mode: the protagonist already woke up. If episode 1 finds them sneering at the regime, the recipe is broken — the awakening is the SHOW, not the backstory.
- Common failure mode: the regime as straw man. If the Regime's Face is a sadist with no argument, the genre's political weight collapses. Aunt Lydia, President Snow, O'Brien — they have to be ALMOST right.
- Common failure mode: the Memory character escapes intact. Knowledge survives, but the bearer pays. If the Memory's grandparent walks off into the sunset with the protagonist, the recipe has slid.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Tonal register — YA-kinetic (Hunger Games, Maze Runner, Divergent), pulp-class-violence (Snowpiercer, Equilibrium), prestige-grim (1984, Handmaid's Tale), prestige-elegiac (Children of Men, Silo), stylized-polemic (V for Vendetta, A Clockwork Orange).
- The Regime's substrate — theocratic (Handmaid's, Equilibrium), totalitarian-secular (1984, V), class-stratified (Hunger Games, Snowpiercer, Silo), eugenic-tier (Divergent, Brave New World), media-capital (Black Mirror, Fahrenheit 451).
- The protagonist's obedient role — soldier, citizen, handmaid, miner, conductor, official, student. Each register dial.
- Setting compression — single building (Silo), single train (Snowpiercer), single dome (Logan's Run), country (Handmaid's, Hunger Games), city (1984, V), labyrinth (Maze Runner).
- The ending register — total defeat (1984, Children of Men), partial revolt (Hunger Games at season end, V), regime crack (Handmaid's late seasons), private refusal (Equilibrium's quieter moments). All conform.
- The Disruptor's substrate — lover, sibling, friend, voice on radio, defector, child. Each register dial.
- How explicit the political argument — V's pamphlet-direct, Hunger Games' allegorical, 1984's philosophical, Children of Men's atmospheric. Range is permitted.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (obedience creates leverage; withholding preserves truth and creates exposure; memory enables defection; coercion opens the public sign) — every ritual, record, route, and symbol varies freely.

## Caveats
- Critical register dial: AWAKENING LEADS TO DEFEAT vs AWAKENING LEADS TO REVOLT. Both endings are valid. Winston in 1984 wakes up and is broken; Theo in Children of Men wakes up and dies handing off a child; Katniss wakes up and the revolution starts. The recipe's FLOOR is the awakening. The revolt may be one gesture (V's exploded Parliament with London still recovering), one transmission, one saved life — or a generalized uprising. Choose the register deliberately; cheap victory is the more common failure.
- Genre proximity: post-apocalyptic shares some tropes (oppression, scarcity, resistance) but has no engineered ideology — survival is the engine, not the regime's logic. If there's no broadcast / ceremony / regime articulating an ideology, the slot is wrong.
- Register dial: YA dystopian (Hunger Games, Divergent, Maze Runner) leans on visible escalation and big set pieces; literary dystopian (1984, Handmaid's, Silo) leans on compression and small refusals. The 10-episode beat spine works for both — adjust the production scale, not the recipe.
