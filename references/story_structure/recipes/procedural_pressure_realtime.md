# Procedural Pressure (Realtime)

## Slot
A FIXED LOCATION (control room, hospital floor, cockpit, dock, single phone line, single car, single building) and a TICKING CLOCK (hours, minutes, fuel, oxygen, blood units, a body count rising) force a TEAM (or a constrained individual) into continuous improvisation under genuine constraint. Competence is the show's content; the location is the world; the clock is the antagonist.

(Tonal range: 24's pulpy-velocity through Eye in the Sky's ethical-procedural to Chernobyl's institutional-cold and Locke's chamber-piece-art. Same recipe — clock + room + improvisation — across registers from procedural-grit to formal-art-film.)

## Emotional contract
**Clock + room + improvisation.** The audience locks in with a competent team or person, watches the constraint tighten as the clock runs, and gets the specific catharsis of professionalism holding (or breaking) under pressure that would crush most people.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Clock** — A named, visible time constraint — hours of oxygen, minutes to the bomb, units of blood, the patient's vitals, the bus's fuel, the surgical window. Shown explicitly; ticking is part of the soundtrack.
- **The Room** — A fixed primary location the show returns to between every scene — control room, OR, cockpit, car interior, dock, hostage building, phone-booth. Often shot from limited angles to enforce the spatial constraint.
- **The Improvisation Beat** — A piece of equipment or procedure used in a way it wasn't designed for — the air filter rebuilt from socks and tape, the surgical tool improvised from a household object, the comms patched through an unauthorized channel, the explanation given in non-jargon to a panicked civilian. The genre's pleasure beat.
- **The Decision That Can't Be Unmade** — A choice the Lead (or sometimes the Junior) makes that resolves the immediate problem at a permanent cost — a patient triaged away from, a hostage's deal accepted, a kill order signed, a flight path locked in, an evacuation called.

**Supporting tropes (stack 3-6 per work):**
- **The Status Update Loop** — Repeating beats where the team checks each instrument / patient vital / asset position. The room's metronome; the audience learns to read the numbers.
- **The Two-Way Tension** — The Lead is on the phone / radio with both the Authority Above (demanding outcomes) and the field team (reporting reality). The Lead translates between them; the strain is the show's content.
- **The Civilian Translator Scene** — A moment where a team member explains the situation to a non-specialist — patient, family, reporter, junior aide, civilian on the line — in plain language. The audience's exposition; the show's humanism.
- **The Tradecraft Detail** — Specific procedure — the surgical sequence, the comms procedure, the flight checklist, the hostage-negotiation script. Shot precisely; mistakes are visible to those who know.
- **The Junior's Solo Beat** — At least one moment where the Junior is left alone with a critical task — the Lead is on a call, in surgery, on the other line. They hold or break; the show's hope beat lives here.
- **The Failure Cascade** — One small failure triggers two more; the room's problems multiply. The clock accelerates without the antagonist doing anything.
- **The Authority Demands Unethical** — The Authority Above asks for something the room won't do — abandon a patient for triage, fire on the wrong target, leak a file, accept casualties as acceptable losses. The Lead either complies under protest or refuses at career cost.
- **The Fuel / Power / Bandwidth Pressure** — Resources shrink visibly during the runtime — battery dies, fuel low, blood bank empty, comms degrading. The audience watches the gauge.
- **The Outsider's Question** — The Outside Witness asks the naive question the team has stopped asking themselves — "Why are we doing it this way?" — and either reframes the problem or is dismissed (the show's ethical compass moment).
- **The Phone Call to Home** — A team member calls their family, partner, child mid-crisis. Sometimes a goodbye; sometimes a check-in; sometimes interrupted. The genre's domestic pulse.
- **The Body in the Room** — A casualty's body remains in the workspace; the team has to keep working around it. The genre's image for what the work costs.
- **The Whiteboard / Plot Board** — A shared surface — whiteboard, status board, tracking screen, vitals monitor — the team writes on collectively. The show's visual exposition.
- **The Quiet Minute** — A brief lull mid-crisis where the team eats, drinks water, leans against a wall, jokes once. The show's only character beat; the audience falls in love here.
- **The Resolution Without Closure** — The immediate crisis ends; the larger context continues. The patient stabilizes but the disease persists; the hostages are out but the politics remain; the plane lands but the investigation begins.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't cut to subplots outside the room (or outside the immediate ring of rooms). The unity of space is the show's discipline.
- Don't introduce a third-act villain. The antagonist is the clock and the situation; sometimes a human Authority Above; never a surprise mastermind.
- Don't resolve through superhuman skill. The team is competent, not exceptional. The Improvisation Beat is clever, not magical.
- Don't pause the clock for character backstory. Character is revealed in action — how someone takes a coffee, who they call, what they joke about — not in flashback or monologue.
- Don't make the Authority Above cartoonishly evil. They have their own pressures; the friction is institutional, not personal.
- Don't end with the team celebrating cleanly. The Resolution Without Closure is the genre's contract. Someone is still in surgery; the next call is already incoming; the body in the room is still there.
- Don't kill all the casualties to raise stakes. The genre's argument is that the work matters; if no one is saved, the show is nihilism.
- Don't break the timeline. Realtime or near-realtime is the floor. Even when the show isn't literally 24's clock, the diegetic time should be continuous or near-continuous.

## Conformity rules
- The Clock is named, visible, and respected. Cheats — the clock slowing for plot, the bomb that ticks for the runtime regardless of action — break the recipe.
- The Room is the show's center. The camera returns to it between every excursion.
- Improvisation, not exceptionalism. The team is competent and clever; they are not magical.
- The Authority Above is institutional, not personally villainous. Friction comes from incompatible pressures, not malice.
- The Resolution Without Closure is non-negotiable. Clean wins where everyone is fine and goes home break the genre.
- The Junior's growth beat must land. The show's hope arc lives in the next-generation character.
- Tradecraft is specific. The procedures shown are real (or convincingly real); errors are detectable to those who know.
- The Casualty / Patient / Hostage must be humanized. Even briefly. They are not a number.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- The Room's specific environment — hospital, cockpit, control room, dock, single car, single phone line, single building, agency situation room, lifeboat, submarine.
- Tonal register — pulpy-velocity (24), procedural-grit (ER, The Wire S2), institutional-cold (Chernobyl, The Hot Zone), ethical-procedural (Eye in the Sky), chamber-piece-art (Locke, Buried, Phone Booth), heroic-naturalistic (Apollo 13, Sully).
- The Clock's specific mechanism — wall clock, vital signs, fuel gauge, hostage body count, oxygen meter, blood units, the moment a deal expires.
- The team's size — single person on a phone (Phone Booth, Locke, Buried) to dozens of staff (ER, Chernobyl, 24's CTU).
- The professional domain — medicine, aerospace, intelligence, fire / disaster response, hostage negotiation, surgery, naval, nuclear.
- Whether the Authority Above is right or wrong — the genre allows both; the friction is what matters.
- Realtime versus near-realtime — 24 is literal; ER is continuous-shift; Chernobyl Ep 1 is the first hours of the disaster; Locke is a single drive. All count; the unity of time is the floor.
- POV structure — single-room (Locke, Buried, Phone Booth) versus ring-of-rooms (24, Chernobyl, ER) where the camera moves between connected locations but the action is continuous.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (finite resources make competent choices collide; the Authority's deadline splits command; the paid-for improvisation becomes the irreversible method) — every Room, clock, procedure, resource, and permanent cost varies freely.

## Caveats
- Common failure mode: the clock loses tension because the audience knows the team will win. The discipline is that the team does NOT always win — some patients die, some hostages are lost, some procedures fail. The Resolution Without Closure must include real cost; if the show only triages winnable cases, the clock is theatre. (Now encoded in the wiring: E9's triage order and E10's visible residue.)
- Common failure mode: a third-act villain. The genre is procedural — the antagonist is the situation. Introducing a human villain who wants the disaster to escalate (a mastermind, a traitor, a cackling administrator) tips the show into thriller and away from procedural-pressure. The Authority Above is the institutional friction; that's not a villain.
- Common failure mode: cutting away from the Room for backstory or subplot. The unity of space and time is the genre's discipline. Flashbacks, B-plots set in unrelated locations, intercut character life — all dilute the pressure. Character must be revealed in the Room.
- Common failure mode: tradecraft theatre — jargon and beeping equipment without procedural specificity. The genre's competence-porn requires the work to be real. The Improvisation Beats must use the room's actual resources; the Status Update Loop must show real instruments. (Now encoded in the wiring: the bypass, resource board, and status rhythm constrain the climax.)
- Register dial: how stylized is the camera? Chamber-piece-art (Locke, Buried) holds on a single face for long takes. Procedural-grit (ER, The Wire) moves through the room kinetically. Pulpy-velocity (24) splits the screen. All three work; pick the dial per project.
- Register dial: how ethical is the show's argument? Eye in the Sky makes the ethics the explicit content; ER lets it run subtextual; 24 was famously willing to argue for the unethical shortcut. The recipe holds across all three positions; the genre demands the question be present, not a specific answer.
- Antagonist discipline note: when the Authority Above is the friction, their motive must be human and locatable — career risk, political calendar, family pressure, prior trauma, a specific bureaucratic incentive. Not "represents the institution" — wants the promotion, was punished last time, is running for office, doesn't know what the room knows. Biographical and specific.
- Genre-of-one risk: none. The realtime-pressure procedural is a durable commercial slot with film, limited-series, and continuing-series exemplars across decades and disciplines.
