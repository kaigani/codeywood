# Sports Underdog Montage

## Slot
The protagonist or team is overlooked, scarred, or counted out; rigorous training transforms craft (not power); rivals personify what they cannot become; a final structured contest decides whether conviction holds. The catharsis is the moment in the contest where the work shows up — sometimes as victory, sometimes as defeat that proves the same thing.

(Tonal range: earnest-Rocky / Mighty Ducks / Cool Runnings through inspirational-Remember the Titans / Hoosiers to prestige-grim Friday Night Lights / Million Dollar Baby. Same engine — training + setback + contest — at registers from crowd-pleaser to elegiac.)

## Emotional contract
**Training + setback + victory.** The audience falls in love with a counted-out protagonist or team, watches the work take real cost (sweat, time, money, relationships, body), and pays off in a final structured contest where the work either wins or wins-by-losing. The cocktail is grit + camaraderie + earned crescendo. Even the losses feel like victories because the contest revealed who they became.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (the engine):**
- **The Training Montage.** Time-compressed sequence of work — sweat, drills, repetition, body changing. Music does heavy lifting. The audience FEELS the time passing.
- **The Coach's Method.** A signature, often eccentric or controversial training method (Mr. Miyagi's wax-on, Rocky's frozen-meat, Hoosiers' four-passes-before-the-shot, Coach Carter's reading-list). The method IS the philosophy.
- **The Setback.** Mid-story, a serious failure — injury, defeat, suspension, loss of sponsor, betrayal, the death. The Underdog's spirit breaks. Without the Setback, no comeback.
- **The Final Contest.** Structured climactic match / game / fight / race / performance with audience, stakes, rules, opponent. The genre's payoff venue.
- **The Crowd's Roar.** At the climax, the diegetic crowd inverts — from indifferent or hostile to roaring with the Underdog. The audience-in-the-theater joins.

**Supporting tropes (stack 5-10 per work):**
- **The Gym (or Equivalent).** The training space — boxing gym, school yard, frozen pond, garage, dance studio. Specific, lived-in, character.
- **The Old Tape / Old Film.** The Coach shows the Underdog footage of the Rival (or the past). Information is delivered through watching.
- **The First Loss.** Before the Final Contest, the Underdog loses a smaller bout. Establishes the gap; sets the climb.
- **The Theme Song.** The work has a signature score — Rocky's horns, Karate Kid's "You're the Best," Cool Runnings' "I Feel Good." The song lives outside the film.
- **The Sponsor / Promoter.** A figure who funds, books, or stands in the way. Sometimes ally, sometimes obstacle.
- **The Wounded Hand / Body.** The Underdog (or a teammate) has a specific physical wound that the contest reopens or risks. Body cost is visible.
- **The Pep Talk.** The Coach (or the Underdog themselves) delivers a single sentence that the audience will remember. The genre's argument compressed.
- **The Locker Room.** Pre-game space; the team is named, established, given small moments. The audience knows everyone.
- **The National Anthem (or equivalent).** A moment of ceremony before the contest — flags, names announced, formal entry. The stakes become public.
- **The Slow-Mo Hit / Throw / Run.** A single moment in the contest stretched into focus. The work shown in one frame.
- **The Crowd Shot.** Faces in the stands — Loved One, Coach, Skeptic, town. The world watching.
- **The Loss-That-Is-Victory.** The Underdog loses the contest but wins what they were actually fighting for (Rocky going the distance, Rudy getting the field, Million Dollar Baby's complicated end).

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't have the Underdog win because of innate talent that surfaced. The work must be visible; talent without the training reads cheap.
- Don't skip the Setback. A linear training-to-victory arc breaks the engine. The audience needs the broken-spirit moment to make the comeback hit.
- Don't have the Coach be flawless. Flawless mentors flatten — the Coach's past failure is what makes their belief in the Underdog real.
- Don't have the Rival redeem morally as the central arc. The Rival can acknowledge the Underdog as peer (post-contest handshake) but does not become a friend within the contest's frame.
- Don't have the Loved One leave the story without consequence. If the Loved One leaves, their absence must be felt in the climax.
- Don't resolve the Past Loss with no contest connection. The hidden stake must be addressed by the final contest — directly or by transformation.
- Don't have the system fully reform. Some Skeptics convert, some don't. The system persists; the Underdog changed within it.
- Don't have the contest end in a sloppy outcome. Win, loss, or loss-that-is-victory — the contest's result must be precise.

## Conformity rules
- The work must be visible. Training Montages and the Coach's Method are non-negotiable. Audiences need to see the time and the sweat.
- The Setback is mandatory. Without the spirit-breaking moment, the comeback has no weight. Even prestige-grim variants (FNL, Million Dollar Baby) hit this beat hard.
- The Final Contest must be structured — named rules, named opponent, audience, stakes. Vague climaxes break the engine.
- The Past Loss must connect to the contest. The audience needs the hidden stake to make the win or loss-that-is-victory specific.
- The Coach must have past failure visible. Flawless mentors flatten the genre; the Coach's belief in the Underdog must be costed in their own history.
- The team / crew must be present and named. Locker Room scenes give the audience the world worth fighting for.
- Body / craft cost must be shown. The Wounded Hand, the broken nose, the lost weight, the bleeding feet. Sport without body is a different genre.
- The contest's outcome must be precise — win, clean loss, or named loss-that-is-victory. Sloppy resolutions break the contract.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Sport — boxing (Rocky, Million Dollar Baby, Cinderella Man, Creed), football (FNL, Remember the Titans, Rudy, We Are Marshall, The Express), basketball (Hoosiers, Coach Carter, White Men Can't Jump), hockey (Mighty Ducks), bobsled (Cool Runnings), baseball (A League of Their Own, Field of Dreams), martial arts (Karate Kid). Non-sport equivalents (Whiplash for drums) work if they hit the spine.
- Solo or team — solo Underdog (Rocky, Million Dollar Baby, Karate Kid, Rudy) vs team Underdog (Mighty Ducks, Cool Runnings, Hoosiers, Remember the Titans). Same engine; team adds Crew dynamics.
- Tonal register — earnest-crowd-pleaser (Cool Runnings, Mighty Ducks, Rocky II), inspirational-uplift (Remember the Titans, Hoosiers, Coach Carter), prestige-grim (FNL, Million Dollar Baby, Whiplash), elegiac-true-story (We Are Marshall, Cinderella Man).
- The system's dismissal — race (Remember the Titans, The Express, Coach Carter), class (Rocky, Cinderella Man, Rudy), gender (A League of Their Own, Million Dollar Baby), age (Rocky later, Creed), body (Rudy's size), geography (Hoosiers, Cool Runnings), institution (Whiplash). Pick the specific dismissal.
- Coach archetype — fallen-pro (Rocky's Mickey, Eastwood in Million Dollar Baby), idealist (Hoosiers' Coach Dale, Remember the Titans' Boone), eccentric (Mr. Miyagi, Cool Runnings' Irv), brutalist (Whiplash's Fletcher). All canonical.
- Final outcome — clean win (Karate Kid, Cool Runnings's symbolic finish, Mighty Ducks), clean loss (Rocky I's loss-that-is-victory), tragic loss (Million Dollar Baby), ambiguous (Whiplash). Pick and commit.
- True-story or fictional — true-story (Rudy, Remember the Titans, Cool Runnings, We Are Marshall, Cinderella Man, The Express) tightens the constraint set; fictional (Rocky, Karate Kid, Hoosiers's composite) allows more invention.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the first loss feeds the crackdown; the setback consumes the body's cost and the home's strain; the final contest consumes the vow, the wound, and the adapted plan) — the CONTENT of every edge varies freely: which institution cracks down, what the wound is, who leaves the seat empty, what technique finally holds.

## Caveats
- The genre is one of the most formula-bound in commercial storytelling. Audiences cheering for the formula is the point — variation rules are narrow.
- Common failure mode: skipping or rushing the Setback. The spirit-breaking middle is the genre's structural load-bearing wall. Cutting it for pace breaks the engine. (Now encoded in the wiring: E8 cashes in E4's body toll, E6's stolen hours, E3's promise, and E1's home strain.)
- Common failure mode: the Underdog wins through innate talent. Talent without visible training reads as cheap. Even pulp-Rocky shows the sweat.
- Common failure mode: the Rival as cartoon villain. The Rival can be unpleasant (Apollo's pre-Rocky arrogance, Daniel-san's Cobra Kai opponents) but must be a credible competitor — credible in skill, real as a person.
- Common failure mode: the Coach as flawless wisdom-dispenser. The Coach's past failure must be visible and connected to the Underdog's stakes.
- Common failure mode: a Final Contest with vague rules. The audience needs the structure - named opponent, named stakes, named rules - to invest.
- The Loss-That-Is-Victory is the genre's prestige register. Rocky going the distance, Rudy getting the field, Frankie's choice in Million Dollar Baby. Used carefully, it's the most cathartic ending available; used lazily, it reads as ducking the win.
- The serialized variant (FNL series, Mighty Ducks: Game Changers) extends the spine across a season but still hits the beats — early Setback, mid-season Return, Finale Final Contest.
- This recipe overlaps with biopic (when true-story), with coming-of-age (when the Underdog is young), and with romance (when the Loved One subplot is large). Distinguish by the engine: sports underdog runs on training + setback + contest. Strip the visible training and structured contest, and it's a different genre.
- Project-specific note: the recipe is FLOOR. Persona-driven polemic colors the system's dismissal (class, race, gender, geography), the Coach's specific past, the Past Loss's texture. But the recipe's core — work + setback + structured contest — cannot be removed.
