# Political Thriller

## Slot
A PROTAGONIST embedded in or against a POWER STRUCTURE (state, agency, party, corporation acting as state) navigates a CONSPIRACY whose stakes escalate from personal to institutional to civic / geopolitical. The protagonist's small choices have outsized consequences; the antagonist is the structure itself, instantiated through named human agents; the truth, when surfaced, does not always win — and when it does, victory costs reputation, relationship, or position.

(Tonal range: The West Wing's satirical-Hill through Borgen's procedural-political-soap to Slow Horses' weary-comic and Tinker Tailor's procedural-cold. Same recipe — conspiracy + escalation + crisis — across registers from idealist-bright to bone-tired.)

## Emotional contract
**Conspiracy + escalation + crisis.** The audience tracks competent professionals navigating a structure they understand better than the audience does, watches the personal cost rise as the stakes climb, and feels the queasy thrill of a small choice in a back room tipping something much larger.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Brief** — Early-season exposition scene where the Operator (and the audience) are told the official version of the situation. The audience must believe this is the truth before it is undermined.
- **The Anomaly** — The small detail that doesn't fit the official version — an asset reporting in too early, a deposit in a wrong account, a name in the cables that shouldn't be there, a witness who saw something they shouldn't have. The conspiracy's first thread.
- **The Tradecraft Procedure** — Specific work scenes — surveillance, cable-drafting, dead drops, talking points, cabinet meetings, leak management. The competence-porn that grounds the show.
- **The Escalation Curve** — The conspiracy's stakes step up across the season — personal (one death) → institutional (a department compromised) → civic / geopolitical (an election, a war, a regime). The curve is the season's tempo.

**Supporting tropes (stack 3-6 per work):**
- **The Backchannel** — A non-official conversation (drinks, dog-walk, private flight, embassy garden) where the real exchange happens. Sometimes between the Operator and the Adversary; sometimes between the Patron and the Adversary, witnessed by the Operator. The genre's argument about how power actually moves.
- **The Cabinet / Situation Room** — A scene in the institutional decision space — Oval Office, cabinet room, COBRA, the desk at the agency, the embassy SCIF. Where the Brief is given and where decisions are formalized.
- **The Leak Beat** — A piece of information goes to the press / a foreign service / a politician. Sometimes the Operator leaks; sometimes the leak is the conspiracy's move. Consequences cascade for two to four episodes.
- **The Burned Source** — A Source / Asset is exposed and killed, extracted, or imprisoned. The Operator either failed to protect them or made the choice that exposed them. The cost beat lands hard.
- **The Loyalty Test** — The Operator is asked (by the Patron, by the Adversary, by their own conscience) to choose between the job and the truth. Usually the test recurs — first time small, second time large.
- **The Family in the Crossfire** — The Civilian / Tether is targeted by the conspiracy or by the institution — surveilled, threatened, leveraged. The Operator's home becomes a tactical problem.
- **The False Friend** — A colleague the Operator trusts is revealed to be the mole / leak / Adversary's asset. Always inside the established cast; never a new face at the reveal.
- **The Press Scene** — A briefing, an interview, a leaked story breaking on cable news. Public reality versus the back-room reality the audience knows.
- **The International Beat** — A scene set in a country other than the show's home — a foreign capital, an embassy, a border, a safe house abroad. The geopolitical scope made geographic.
- **The Resignation Threat** — The Operator (or the Patron) threatens to walk away from the job. The threat is real; sometimes it pays off mid-season; sometimes it's leveraged into a deeper compromise.
- **The Vote / Decision Clock** — A specific institutional deadline — a vote, a state visit, a summit, an election night, a UN resolution — anchors the season's third-act tempo.
- **The Compromised Senior** — A senior official (Patron-adjacent or above) is revealed to be part of the conspiracy. The Operator must navigate around them while still appearing inside the institution.
- **The Public Speech** — A scene of public address (parliament, press conference, town hall, op-ed) where the truth is named in part. Sometimes the Operator's final move; sometimes the Adversary's parry.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't end with the institution destroyed. The genre's argument is that institutions persist. They may be reformed, embarrassed, or shaken; they do not fall. (One head of the conspiracy may; the structure does not.)
- Don't redeem the Adversary through a sudden conscience moment. They may withdraw; they may even respect the Operator; they do not become an ally except as a tactical defection.
- Don't make the Operator combat-competent. They are clever, embedded, professional. Gunfights are rare and ugly. When violence happens, it's brief and consequential.
- Don't explain the conspiracy in a monologue. The audience earns the truth through cables, scenes, photographs, leaked documents. The Operator pieces it together; the audience pieces it together with them.
- Don't make all politicians venal. The genre survives on the audience being able to distinguish the conspirator from the merely careerist or the actually principled. A show where everyone is corrupt is satire, not thriller.
- Don't kill the Operator. They may end the season disgraced, exiled, or reassigned — but the genre's contract is that the Operator survives to continue the work (or to be replaced by their successor in a parallel arc).
- Don't let the Civilian / Tether be untouched. The Operator's home life pays a price. A finale where the family is intact and unaware is a failure of the recipe.
- Don't introduce the True Villain at the eleventh hour. The Adversary must be in the show's cast from at least episode three.

## Conformity rules
- The Operator must be embedded in the structure they are investigating. Outside-the-system protagonists are journalism thrillers — adjacent recipe, different genre.
- The Adversary must be human and named. The conspiracy is a structure; its agents are people the audience can read.
- The Escalation Curve is non-negotiable. Stakes step from personal to institutional to civic / geopolitical across the season.
- The tradecraft must be specific. Cables, surveillance, briefings, votes, polls, dead drops — the work is shown.
- The Civilian / Tether pays a real price. A finale where the family is intact, untouched, and unaware breaks the contract.
- The institution persists. One head falls; the body continues. Reformist hope is allowed; revolutionary closure is not.
- The audience earns the truth through accumulation. No monologue exposition of the full conspiracy; the truth surfaces as the Operator surfaces it.
- The Adversary is competent enough to survive at least one round. A villain caught in act two breaks the season's escalation.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- The institutional setting — intelligence agency (Slow Horses, Tinker Tailor, Tehran), foreign service / embassy (The Diplomat, Night Manager), parliament / cabinet (Borgen, House of Cards), executive office (The West Wing), corporate-state hybrid (Mr Robot), security service (Bodyguard).
- Tonal register — satirical-Hill (West Wing), procedural-political-soap (Borgen, House of Cards), weary-comic (Slow Horses, Patriot), procedural-cold (Tinker Tailor, Tehran), high-stakes-action-adjacent (Bodyguard, Homeland).
- The geopolitical theatre — Cold War, post-9/11 counterterror, contemporary diplomatic, near-future cyber, party-political domestic, corporate-state.
- The Operator's specific role — analyst, ambassador, MP, spy, journalist-with-access, special agent, junior cabinet officer.
- The Adversary's domain — foreign service, opposition party, corporate fixer, organized crime as state proxy, internal faction.
- The Civilian / Tether's relation — spouse, child, ex, sibling, civilian friend, journalist-source-turned-personal.
- Single-season vs multi-season — limited-series concentrates the curve (Tehran S1, Night Manager); continuing series spreads it across multiple conspiracies with cumulative cost (Slow Horses, Homeland).
- The wiring's edges are fixed (institutional trace and private omission burn the Source; desk disloyalty exposes the home; accumulated records force only a partial truth) — every office, document, deadline, and personal price varies freely.

## Caveats
- Common failure mode: the conspiracy expands to include everyone, becoming undifferentiated. The genre's intelligence is in distinguishing the conspirator from the careerist from the principled. A show where everyone is corrupt is satire (Veep, In the Loop); a show where everyone is heroic is fantasy (West Wing at its most idealistic). The recipe lives in the middle — most people are working their job, some are venal, some are conspiring, and the Operator has to tell them apart.
- Common failure mode: tradecraft theatre without political substance — surveillance, dead drops, and cables that don't add up to a coherent institutional argument. The genre demands the conspiracy mean something about how power works; the work is procedure but the show is argument.
- Common failure mode: a clean win in the finale. The Operator exposes the conspiracy, the Adversary is jailed, the institution is reformed, the Tether returns. This is fantasy, not thriller. The recipe demands cost — the win is partial and the price is real.
- Common failure mode: the Civilian / Tether as decoration. If the family appears only to be missed at dinners, the cost beat is hollow. The Tether must be a person with their own arc and their own breaking point.
- Register dial: how much hope? West Wing argues institutions can be redeemed; Tinker Tailor argues they can only be survived. Both work; pick the dial per project. The midline (Slow Horses, Borgen) is the most populated commercial slot.
- Antagonist discipline note: the Adversary's motive must be human and locatable — career advancement, ideological conviction, personal grudge, family loyalty, a specific old defeat. Not "represents the deep state" — wants the directorship, was passed over in '03, blames the West for his father's death. Biographical, specific.
- Genre-of-one risk: none. The slot has been continuously commercial for sixty years across film, novel, and television in multiple languages.
