# Domestic Horror (Intimacy + Intrusion + Violation)

## Slot
Serialized horror where the threat is INSIDE the home — the marriage, the family, the parent-child bond, the partner sleeping next to you. The protagonist's safety architecture (relationship, house, routine, child) is the site of the horror; what was supposed to protect them is what hurts them. The genre's argument is that intimacy is the deepest vector for violation. Pays in the slow recognition that the locked door was locked from the inside.

## Corpus
- *Hereditary* (Aster)
- *Get Out* (Peele)
- *The Babadook* (Kent)
- *Goodnight Mommy* (Franz/Fiala)
- *Relic* (James)
- *Possessor* (Cronenberg, B.)
- *Run* (Chaganty)
- *Mother!* (Aronofsky)
- *We Need to Talk About Kevin* (Ramsay)
- *Sharp Objects* (HBO / Flynn)
- *Servant* (Shyamalan)
- *The Stepfather* (Ruben)
- *Big Little Lies* (HBO, drama-leaning)
- *I Saw the TV Glow* (Schoenbrun)

(Tonal range: Hereditary's elevated-prestige and Mother!'s art-allegorical through The Babadook's metaphor-grief and Big Little Lies' drama-thriller hybrid to Run's pulpy-Lifetime-thriller and The Stepfather's TV-movie. Same recipe — the home is the site, the loved one is the wound.)

## Emotional contract
**Intimacy + violation + recognition.** The audience watches a protagonist's domestic life — partner, child, parent, sibling, house — slowly reveal itself as the source of the horror. The cocktail is the queasy slide from "something is wrong here" to "the person I love has been wrong all along" (or "I have been wrong"). Catharsis is recognition, often arriving too late. Comfort returning to normal is not on the menu.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Domestic Wrongness** — Small first signal — a half-eaten meal in a strange place, a partner who didn't sleep, a child's drawing, a closet that smells wrong. Tiny, deniable, repeating. The audience clocks; the protagonist explains away.
- **The Locked Door from Inside** — The horror cannot be expelled because it lives INSIDE the safety architecture. Calling 911 calls home. Leaving means destroying the family. The constraint that makes domestic horror itself.
- **The Loved One's Lucid Moment** — Mid-season: the Intimate Threat has one window of clarity — speaks normally, asks for help, says the thing — before going back under. The protagonist's deepest cut.
- **The Outside Eye's Warning** — Friend / sister / doctor names the threat directly. The protagonist defends the home. The warning is filed and proven right at unbearable cost.
- **The Recognition** — Climactic scene where the protagonist sees what they have been refusing to see. Usually quiet, often domestic — at the kitchen table, in a hallway, looking at an old photograph. The horror's whole architecture lands at once.

**Supporting tropes:**
- **The Family Photograph** — A photo, album, home video that contains a face, posture, or scene that confirms the inheritance. Often discovered during a routine task (cleaning, packing, sorting).
- **The Dinner Scene** — Set-piece family meal where the dynamics surface — through silence, a wrong remark, a child's question, a spouse's coldness. The genre's signature image.
- **The Locked Room in the House** — A space the protagonist hasn't gone in — attic, basement, the Threat's office, the dead relative's room. When entered, the inheritance crystallizes.
- **The Ritual of Care** — A daily intimacy (feeding, bathing, dressing, medicating, putting to bed) that is shown three times and then becomes the site of horror. Routine weaponized.
- **The Therapist / Doctor Visit** — Professional outsider takes a session, names something the protagonist can't accept; protagonist switches doctors or sabotages the appointment.
- **The Wedding Photo / Honeymoon Memory** — Sentimental object the protagonist clings to as proof the marriage / family was good. By Ep 8 it reads differently in the audience's eye.
- **The Child's Drawing** — Crayon picture, model, school project that depicts something the child shouldn't know. Innocent vector for revelation.
- **The Neighbor Who Watches** — Adjacent household with their own opinion; either Outside Eye (helps) or Co-Conspirator (closes the loop). The audience reads the neighborhood politics.
- **The Returning Trauma** — A past event (a death, a miscarriage, an affair, an abuse) that resurfaces in the present and rewrites the safety architecture retroactively.
- **The Failed Confidant** — Protagonist tries to tell someone — a parent, a priest, a 911 call — and the confidant minimizes, redirects, or sides with the Intimate Threat. Confirms the locked door from inside.
- **The House Behaves** — The physical home contributes — doors that swell shut, lights that flicker over the wrong room, a stain that returns, a smell, a sound from a wall. The home is the second body of the threat.
- **The Identity Confusion** — Self-as-threat variant — gaps in memory, a face in the mirror that's wrong, finding evidence of acts the protagonist doesn't remember (Sharp Objects, Possessor, Goodnight Mommy).

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't introduce the threat from outside. A home invasion thriller is the WRONG genre — domestic horror requires the threat to be already inside the safety architecture, by blood or marriage or co-residence.
- Don't make the Intimate Threat a cartoon monster. They must be loveable in the first act. The audience must understand why the protagonist married / mothered / cared for them.
- Don't let the protagonist call the police and have them help. Authority must fail — minimizes the report, sides with the Threat, arrives too late, is part of the inheritance.
- Don't resolve with the home restored. Either the home is destroyed (literally or metaphorically), the protagonist leaves at unbearable cost, or the protagonist stays and is changed. Reset to normal is the wrong genre.
- Don't kill the Vulnerable Stake without weight. Their harm must be the season's defining loss; quick or casual death belongs to monster horror.
- Don't reveal the inheritance through monologue. The protagonist must ASSEMBLE it from the family photograph, the locked room, the Outside Eye's warnings, the child's drawing.
- Don't make the Outside Eye omniscient. Their warnings are partial, suspicious-sounding, easy to dismiss in the moment. Their rightness is retroactive.

## Conformity rules
- The threat must be inside the safety architecture from the start. Home-invasion stories belong to thriller; domestic horror requires intimacy as the vector.
- The Intimate Threat must be loveable in Episode 1. The audience needs to believe the marriage / family was real before the horror surfaces.
- The protagonist's denial must be SHOWN as defensible. Each red flag explained-away must be plausible. The genre is the cost of love-shaped attention.
- Authority must fail — police, doctors, family elders, social services. The protagonist cannot be rescued by institutions.
- The Locked Door must be from inside — the threat cannot be expelled without destroying the home.
- The Recognition scene must be quiet and domestic. Loud action belongs to thriller.
- The home must not be restored at the end. Domestic horror does not reset.

- Failure mode: the Intimate Threat as obvious villain from frame 1. If the audience knows the spouse is a killer in the cold open, the genre collapses into thriller. The threat must be loveable first.
- Failure mode: rescue by authority. Police arriving in time defeats the recipe. Domestic horror requires institutional failure.
- Failure mode: the home reset at the end. Domestic horror never goes back to normal. Either the unit is destroyed or the protagonist is permanently changed.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Threat relation — partner, child, parent, sibling, in-law, household. Each shifts the genre's specific grammar.
- Threat origin — possession, illness (genetic / psychiatric), ideology / cult, supernatural inheritance, identity (closeted, dissociative), criminal hidden life. Each is a register dial.
- Protagonist's role in the home — mother, wife, husband, adult child, caregiver, parent of a teenager. Each shifts denial and stake.
- Whether the protagonist is partly the threat (self-as-threat variant: Sharp Objects, Possessor, Goodnight Mommy). Different beat-7/8 grammar but same recipe.
- The home itself — house, apartment, multi-generational compound, gated community, rural farmhouse, urban condo. Architecture as register.
- Tonal register — elevated-prestige (Hereditary, Sharp Objects), allegorical (Mother!), pulpy thriller (Run, The Stepfather), grief-metaphor (The Babadook), drama-hybrid (Big Little Lies). Recipe constant.
- Era — contemporary default; period possible (gothic variants), near-future (technology-as-intimate-vector).
- The wiring's edges are fixed (denial supplies authority's case; the altered care ritual harms the Stake; domestic artifacts assemble the Recognition; inside knowledge enables the Cost) — every relation, artifact, ritual, and final price varies freely.

## Caveats
- Register dial: prestige slows the Domestic Wrongness across two episodes of careful, almost-quiet observation; pulpy compresses the inheritance-reveal into Ep 3. Both work.
- Genre proximity: home-invasion thriller has the threat from outside (Funny Games, Hush); domestic horror has the threat from inside (Hereditary, Get Out). The line is whether the protagonist married / birthed / raised / lived with the threat.
- Possible drift: the protagonist becoming the threat (Sharp Objects, Possessor, Goodnight Mommy) is a valid variant but shifts the Outside Eye into greater prominence — they may become the show's POV in the third act.
