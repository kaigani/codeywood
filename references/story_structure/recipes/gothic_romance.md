# Gothic Romance (Mysterious House / Mysterious Lover)

## Slot
Serialized romance-horror where a protagonist of modest means or fragile circumstance is drawn into the orbit of a MYSTERIOUS FIGURE inside a SPECIFIC HOUSE (or estate, theatre, ship, manor) whose past contains a buried wrong. Desire pulls the protagonist deeper; suspicion tells them to leave; revelation forces them to choose what they will be on the other side of knowing. Atmosphere is candlelight, weather, locked rooms, and the slow erosion of disbelief.

## Corpus
- *Jane Eyre* (Brontë)
- *Rebecca* (du Maurier / Hitchcock)
- *Wuthering Heights* (Brontë)
- *Crimson Peak* (del Toro)
- *Phantom Thread* (Anderson)
- *Beauty and the Beast* (Cocteau / Disney / del Toro's *Shape of Water*)
- *Bram Stoker's Dracula* (Coppola)
- *The Phantom of the Opera* (Leroux / Lloyd Webber / Schumacher)
- *Northanger Abbey* (Austen)
- *The Essex Serpent* (Apple TV / Perry)
- *The Haunting of Bly Manor* (Flanagan)
- *The Beguiled* (Coppola)
- *The Others* (Amenábar, lean)
- *Lady Chatterley's Lover* (Lawrence, lean)
- *The Piano* (Campion)

(Tonal range: Jane Eyre's candle-lit-melancholy and The Others' chilled-restraint through Phantom Thread's couture-control and Bly Manor's prestige-tender to Crimson Peak's del-Toro-lush and Dracula's full-operatic. Same recipe — pull toward a figure who keeps a locked room.)

## Emotional contract
**Desire + suspicion + revelation.** The audience is seduced alongside the protagonist, then made to hold dread on their behalf, then forced to watch what the protagonist becomes when the locked room opens. Catharsis is the protagonist choosing — not being saved. Sentiment without horror reads as romance; horror without sentiment reads as gothic without the romance. The cocktail requires both at full strength.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Locked Room** — A specific door, wing, attic, cellar, or cabinet the Protagonist is told never to enter. The season's structural axis. By the end she enters; what she finds rearranges her.
- **The Predecessor's Shadow** — The previous wife / sister / lover whose presence permeates the House. Her portrait, her clothes, her perfume, her piano, her dog. Romantic rival who isn't there.
- **The Storm Night** — The House cut off from the outside world by weather, distance, or social rule. Phones dead, roads flooded, train missed, ball ending. Forced proximity to the Figure with no escape valve.
- **The Revelation Scene** — The locked room opens. The Protagonist learns what the Figure has been hiding — body, child, contract, debt, prior crime. The exact midpoint at which desire and horror become the same emotion.
- **The Choice Under the Portrait** — Late-season scene under the Predecessor's painted gaze where the Protagonist decides to stay, leave, or destroy. The genre's signature image.

**Supporting tropes:**
- **The Foreboding Welcome** — Arrival by carriage / car / train at dusk; the Housekeeper meets her; the House is too large; her room is too far from his.
- **The Wedding That Comes Too Fast** — Engagement, marriage, or commitment occurs before the Protagonist understands the household. The legal binding precedes the knowing.
- **The Portrait That Watches** — A painting that the Protagonist feels looked at by; later turns out to be the Predecessor, an ancestor, or a self-portrait.
- **The Dress That Was Hers** — Garment, jewel, perfume, or song that belonged to the Predecessor; the Protagonist is offered or tricked into wearing it.
- **The Library Scene** — Late-night private conversation between Protagonist and Figure beside fire / shelves / piano. Confession without full disclosure. The seduction's intellectual phase.
- **The Servants' Whispers** — Overheard fragments — a name, a date, "she" — that the Protagonist cannot place until later.
- **The Walk on the Grounds** — Outdoor scene of intimacy and disclosure — moor, garden, cliff, beach. The House's exterior gives them permission the interior denies.
- **The Old Diary / Letter** — Found document in the Predecessor's hand. Half the truth, in the dead woman's voice.
- **The Doctor's Visit** — A medical professional arrives; the Protagonist begins to wonder if she is losing her mind or being poisoned. Gaslight pressure on the senses.
- **The Fire / The Flood** — The House's reckoning expressed through element. Manderley burns; Crimson Peak's red clay rises; Bly's lake rises. Atmosphere becomes verdict.
- **The Faithful Confidant Arrives Too Late (or Just in Time)** — The Safe Suitor / loyal friend appears in the third act. Their function is to be denied or used, depending on the Protagonist's choice.
- **The Touch That Lingers** — A specific physical exchange — hand on a hand, glove removed, hair touched — that the Protagonist replays. The genre's restraint signature.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't make the Figure cruel without seduction. If he is just a villain, the audience never feels the pull. The danger must be entangled with desire.
- Don't make the Protagonist purely innocent. She must want something — to be seen, to be loved, to leave her old life, to know — and the House offers it.
- Don't explain the secret in dialogue early. The Locked Room must be approached by stages — portrait, whisper, diary, dress, dream — and entered late.
- Don't let the Predecessor be simple. She is wronged AND dangerous, victim AND warning. The ambiguity is the genre's center.
- Don't resolve the romance into comfortable domesticity. Even at the happy ending the House marks them. They survive but altered.
- Don't make the Housekeeper / Guardian a snob in service of class comedy. She must believe — in the House, in the past, in the order. Her devotion is religious.
- Don't set the climax in daylight at a public function. The genre's reckoning happens in the House, with weather.

## Conformity rules
- The House must be specific, named, mappable, and recurring as image — its corridors, its weather, its locked door must be drawn the same way every episode.
- The Mysterious Figure must be desirable. If the audience is not pulled, the Locked Room is just a crime scene. Charisma is the recipe's first ingredient.
- The Locked Room must be set up by Episode 2 and entered by Episode 8. Any earlier and the suspense collapses; any later and the audience disengages.
- The Predecessor's presence must be felt before her facts are known. Portrait, dress, scent, song — atmosphere first, biography second.
- The Servant Who Knows must be punished for telling. The class cost is the genre's moral underline.
- The revelation must rearrange the Protagonist, not just explain the plot. If she ends the season the same person, the genre is mystery, not gothic romance.
- The climax must occur in or around the House, with weather. Daylight public resolution is the wrong genre.

- Failure mode: making the Figure a clear villain too early. The audience must want him for the genre to work. If revelation arrives in Episode 2 the rest is just regret.
- Failure mode: the Protagonist as passive recipient. She must investigate — open the diary, follow the corridor, ask the servant, refuse the dress. Gothic romance requires hunger plus initiative.
- Failure mode: tonal collapse into either pure romance (sentimental) or pure horror (clinical). The cocktail requires both vectors at once in most scenes; if a scene is purely one, the recipe is leaking.
- Failure mode: the Predecessor as simple victim. She is the audience's projected past — wronged AND warning, both. Flatten her and the locked room is just a room.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Era — Regency (Northanger), Victorian (Jane Eyre, Dracula), Edwardian (Crimson Peak), mid-century (Rebecca, Phantom Thread), contemporary (Bly Manor, The Others). The recipe survives any.
- The Figure's nature — ordinary man with secret (Rochester), supernatural being (Dracula, the Phantom, the Faun, the Amphibian Man), wounded craftsman (Reynolds Woodcock), ghost (Bly), woman (The Beguiled, The Piano). Gender and species both dial.
- Whether the House is haunted literally or atmospherically. Supernatural / gaslight / natural — pick deliberately; the audience reads which one early.
- The Predecessor's truth — wronged victim (Rebecca's inverse), monstrous in her own right (Rebecca herself), still alive (Jane Eyre), the Protagonist's own ancestor (Crimson Peak). The ambiguity belongs to the season.
- The Confidant's role — denied (Jane Eyre's St John), used (Phantom Thread's doctor), rescuer (Northanger), never invoked. Romance shape varies.
- Ending — stay altered / escape wounded / destroy. All three valid; choose for the cocktail you want.
- Register — austere-restrained (The Others, Phantom Thread), prestige-tender (Bly), commercial-romantic (Bridgerton-adjacent gothics), del-Toro-lush, operatic-Coppola, pastoral-Campion. The recipe handles all.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the binding promise enables isolation; the Servant's fragment and punishment open the room; the House's mapped weakness becomes the sovereign choice's method) — every secret, relic, weather event, and ending varies freely.

## Caveats
- Register dial: the supernatural is optional. The Others, Rebecca, and Phantom Thread are gothic without ghosts; Crimson Peak and Bly are gothic with them. The House remembers either way.
- Genre proximity: domestic horror's antagonist is the spouse / family weaponized against the protagonist (gaslight without seduction). Gothic romance keeps desire on the page. If the Figure is only menace, the recipe has drifted to domestic horror or thriller.
