# Tragedy (Hope + Flaw + Ruin)

## Slot
Serialized or single-arc drama in which a PROTAGONIST with a NAMED FLAW pursues something the audience can already see they will not survive intact. The classical engine — "the great man brought low by pride" — is too narrow. The working engine is HOPE: the audience starts the story rooting for the protagonist to avoid a fate the structure has already promised, then watches the flaw do its quiet, patient work. Catharsis is not surprise. Catharsis is having hoped anyway. The recipe runs from Shakespeare to working-class Aronofsky to Lonergan's chamber-quiet.

(Tonal range: Shakespeare's prestige-verse and Antigone's classical-stoic through The Godfather II's operatic-epic and There Will Be Blood's biblical-American to Breaking Bad's prestige-serial, Aronofsky's working-class-grime, A Star Is Born's romantic-melodrama, and Manchester by the Sea's chamber-restraint. Same recipe — the audience hopes, the flaw works, the ruin lands.)

## Emotional contract
**Hope + flaw + ruin.** The audience is shown the protagonist's possibility — talent, love, position, family, freedom, fight — and shown the flaw that is already shaping into the wound. They watch the flaw work over time. They hope it won't. Catharsis is grief for what the protagonist was given and what they could not keep. The recipe demands real possibility at the open; ruin without hope at the start is misery, not tragedy. The recipe demands a flaw that is the protagonist's own, not bad luck; ruin without flaw is misfortune, not tragedy.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Possibility Established** — Early scene(s) that show what the protagonist has — talent, love, position, family, body, freedom, art, command. The recipe spends real time establishing what will be lost. Skipping this beat collapses the cocktail.
- **The Flaw Named** — Specific, nameable, demonstrated in action — not "he was proud" but a scene in which pride costs something small. The flaw must be readable by the audience in Act 1 even if the protagonist refuses to see it.
- **The Hope Beat** — Recurring moments where it looks like the protagonist might pull through — a moment of insight, a love restored, a victory, a chance refused. Each Hope Beat is engineered to be felt as real possibility, then watched as the flaw closes the door.
- **The Point of No Return** — Specific midpoint act after which the ruin becomes structural. Macbeth kills Banquo. Walter watches Jane die. Daniel kills the man at the bowling alley. Randy takes the second hit. The protagonist is still walking but the body is already falling.
- **The Recognition** — Late-stage moment in which the protagonist sees their own flaw or refuses to see it. Aristotle's anagnorisis; the genre's defining beat. Tragedy without Recognition (the protagonist never knows what they did) is the cruelest register; with Recognition the audience watches them understand. Both valid; pick deliberately.

**Supporting tropes:**
- **The Inheritance Frame** — Opening or recurring reference to the older harm — father's death, family's history, country's past, the prior addiction, the immigrant generation. The flaw is shown to be older than the protagonist.
- **The Public Triumph** — Mid-arc moment of seemingly justified achievement — the title fight, the corner of the meth market, the throne taken, the daughter married, the marriage saved. Acts as the Hope Beat at its largest; signals the next Point of No Return is near.
- **The Warning Refused** — A character close to the protagonist names the flaw or its likely cost in one explicit scene. The protagonist hears, hardens, and continues. Skyler to Walter. Lady Macduff's wisdom. The trainer warning the boxer. The doctor warning the addict.
- **The Letter / The Phone Call** — Specific incoming communication that, if the protagonist read or answered correctly, would have changed the outcome. They don't. (Romeo's missed letter. The undelivered message in countless Shakespeare tragedies. Manchester's voicemail.)
- **The Quiet Domestic Scene** — A scene of ordinary love before the ruin — a meal, a child, a bath, a song, a sleep. The recipe demands you see the small life the protagonist is breaking.
- **The Spiral Sequence** — Montage or extended sequence in which the flaw's acceleration is visible — Requiem's split screen, Breaking Bad's lab time-lapses, Macbeth's body count, Wrestler's pill stack. The flaw becomes its own engine.
- **The Innocent Hurt** — The Wronged Innocent suffers a specific harm directly attributable to the protagonist — even by inaction. The audience watches them feel it; the protagonist looks away. The recipe's moral pivot.
- **The Mirror Conversation** — A scene in which the Tempter / Mirror Antagonist articulates the protagonist's own argument back to them — Lady Macbeth's manhood speech, Iago's whisper, Gus's measured voice, the manager's pep talk. The flaw, named by an ally.
- **The Lost Future** — A moment, often visualized or stated, of the life the protagonist could have had — the daughter not yet married, the title kept, the retirement reached, the love returned. Recipe's grief seed.
- **The Final Domestic Image** — Late image — the chair, the table, the bed, the empty room, the photograph — of the household after the ruin. The recipe's grief deposit.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't supply ruin without hope. The audience must be made to want what the protagonist will lose. Misery from frame one is misery porn, not tragedy.
- Don't supply ruin without flaw. If the protagonist is destroyed only by bad luck or villainy, the structure is melodrama or thriller, not tragedy. The protagonist's hand must be in the ruin.
- Don't make the flaw vague. "She was reckless" is description; "She would rather die than ask her father for help" is the engine. Specify.
- Don't redeem the protagonist with a clean third-act turn. The flaw is structural; if it can be cured by sympathy, the season is a recovery arc, not a tragedy. Recognition without rescue is the recipe's tone.
- Don't let the One Who Loved Them be passive scenery. They must try — clearly, recognizably — and their failure must be earned by the flaw's specific resistance.
- Don't moralize. The recipe shows the flaw doing its work; it does not lecture the audience on what to learn. The catharsis is grief, not instruction.
- Don't deny the audience the Quiet Domestic Scene. Even Macbeth keeps his marriage on stage. Without the small life, the ruin is abstract.
- Don't pile on twists. Tragedy is structural inevitability, not surprise. If the third-act reveal changes who the protagonist was, the recipe has drifted to thriller.

## Conformity rules
- The Possibility must be established at full strength. If the audience doesn't want what the protagonist has, the recipe has no fuel.
- The Flaw must be specific, named, and visible in action by Episode 1. Generic flaws ("he was proud," "she had a temper") collapse the engine.
- The Flaw must be the protagonist's own. External destruction without internal contribution is melodrama or thriller. The protagonist's hand must be in the ruin.
- The Hope Beats must be engineered as real. If the audience can't believe in the possibility, the catharsis evaporates. Engineer hope deliberately at three points minimum.
- The Wronged Innocent must suffer specifically. The audience must feel the cost in another body; the protagonist's self-destruction alone is half-recipe.
- The Recognition (or its explicit refusal) must occur. The audience earns the grief by watching the protagonist see or not see.
- The ruin must be structural — earned by the flaw's patient work, not by surprise twist or external accident. Tragedy that depends on plot mechanics is the wrong genre.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Register — prestige-verse (Shakespeare, Antigone), operatic-epic (Godfather II, There Will Be Blood), prestige-serial (Breaking Bad), working-class-grime (Wrestler, Requiem, Mystic River), chamber-restraint (Manchester, A Star Is Born's quiet register), biblical-American (TWBB, von Trier's Jack), romantic-melodrama (A Star Is Born commercial register). Recipe runs all.
- The Flaw — pride (Macbeth, Walter), jealousy (Othello), indecision (Hamlet), grief that cannot let go (Manchester), unprocessed shame (Mystic River), appetite (Daniel, Jack), self-destruction (Randy, Sara), idealism rigidified to inflexibility (Antigone). Same engine, different shapes.
- The Recognition — explicit (Macbeth's "tomorrow and tomorrow"), refused (Iago's silence; Walter's "I liked it"), arrived too late (Othello's last speech), absent (Mystic River's Jimmy never quite). Pick deliberately; the choice tells the audience what the recipe is arguing.
- Ending — death (classical), survival altered (Lee Chandler, Walter, Jake LaMotta), exile, total destruction of legacy (Plainview, Jack). All valid; the recipe's last verdict is dial.
- The Tempter — explicit antagonist (Iago, Gus, Lady Macbeth), amoral catalyst (the manager, the cartel), the protagonist's mirror (Jack, Sara, Randy). Tempter is dial; the recipe's flaw still must be the protagonist's own.
- Scale — single household (Manchester, Mystic River), city (Godfather, Breaking Bad), country (Macbeth, There Will Be Blood), self (Requiem, Wrestler). The recipe scales.
- Era and setting — classical (Shakespeare, Sophocles), period (Godfather, There Will Be Blood), contemporary (most modern entries). Era is dial; the engine is constant.
- Whose POV — protagonist (most), bystander looking in (Mystic River shifts; Nick Carraway's structural lineage), ensemble centered on a doomed figure (A Star Is Born). POV is dial.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the Point of No Return consumes the refused exit and the private bargain; the Ruin consumes the buried deed, the mishandled message, and the gift) — the CONTENT of every edge varies freely: which flaw, what bargain, whose wound, what the exit looked like, what the message said.

## Caveats
- Failure mode: ruin without hope. If Episode 1 starts in the gutter, the audience cannot grieve what they never saw. Spend real time on the Possibility. (Now encoded in the wiring: E1-named-gift must be strong enough that E5 and E10 can spend it.)
- Failure mode: ruin without flaw. If the protagonist is purely a victim, the structure is melodrama. The flaw must be theirs; their hand must be visible in the wreck. (Now encoded in the wiring: every edge into E10 traces back through a protagonist choice — the refused exit, the buried deed, the mishandled message.)
- Failure mode: the flaw cured. The recipe rejects clean third-act redemption. Recognition is permitted; rescue is not. If the protagonist pulls out and the family is restored, the recipe has drifted to recovery drama (a valid genre, not this one).
- Failure mode: moralized voiceover. The recipe shows the flaw working; it does not explain. If the season tells the audience what they should feel, it has stopped being tragedy and started being a sermon.
- Failure mode: misery porn. Pure unrelieved suffering without engineered hope reads as cruelty. The Quiet Domestic Scene and the Hope Beats are recipe-mandatory antidotes; without them tragedy collapses to misery.
- Failure mode: twist endings. Tragedy is structural inevitability. If Episode 10 reveals the protagonist was someone else, the recipe is broken. The audience knew what was coming; the recipe is HOW it came. (Now encoded in the wiring: E10's Needs line contains nothing the audience has not watched being chosen.)
- Failure mode: the protagonist as monster. Walter White works because we watched him become; if he started Heisenberg, the recipe has nothing to do. Even Macbeth opens with the loyal soldier. The flaw begins as gift's shadow, not as villain's mark. (Now encoded in E1: gift at full strength, flaw fired once and small.)
- Register dial: working-class tragedy (Wrestler, Requiem, A Star Is Born's quiet register) and prestige-classical tragedy (Shakespeare, Godfather) are the same recipe at opposite economic scales. The flaw, the hope, the ruin — same shape, different costume.
- Genre proximity: melodrama supplies suffering without specifying the protagonist's flaw; thriller supplies ruin by external mechanism. Tragedy demands BOTH internal flaw and engineered hope. Either missing, the recipe has drifted.
- Genre proximity: literary fiction often borrows tragic structure without the recipe's hope beats — observation without engineered identification. Tragedy as commercial recipe insists on the audience's hope as fuel; if you remove the hope beats for restraint, register risks chamber-literary rather than commercial-tragedy.
