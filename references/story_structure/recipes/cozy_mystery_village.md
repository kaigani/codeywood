# Cozy Mystery Village

## Slot
Episodic or limited-serial mystery where an AMATEUR or semi-amateur SLEUTH solves a murder in a SMALL FIXED COMMUNITY (village, parish, small town, named building) without gore, without sex on-screen, and without the social fabric ultimately tearing. The crime disrupts; the sleuth restores; the village endures.

- *Father Brown* (BBC)
- *Midsomer Murders* (ITV)
- *Hamish Macbeth* (BBC)
- *Murder, She Wrote* (CBS)
- *Only Murders in the Building* (Hulu)
- *The Marlow Murder Club* (UKTV)
- *Endeavour* (ITV)
- *Vera* (ITV)
- *Shetland* (BBC)
- *Pie in the Sky* (BBC)
- *Death Comes to Pemberley* (BBC)
- *Pie in the Sky* (BBC)

- *Grantchester* (ITV)

(Tonal range: Death in Paradise's sunlit-comic through Midsomer's gently-baroque to Shetland's chilly-prestige and Vera's working-class-melancholic. Same recipe — puzzle + community + restoration — across registers from holiday-light to almost-noir.)

## Emotional contract
**Puzzle + community + restoration.** The audience gets the satisfaction of a fair-play whodunit, the comfort of a fixed familiar place with characters they want to revisit, and the reassurance that the social order — eccentric, charming, slightly daft — survives the disruption.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Closed Circle** — All suspects are inside the community. The killer is not a stranger passing through. The audience contracts with the show that everyone who matters is on-screen.
- **The Fair-Play Clue** — Every clue needed to solve the murder is shown to the audience before the reveal. The Sleuth sees it; the audience sees it; the meaning lands at the climax.
- **The Reputation Reversal** — The Victim's public image inverts during the investigation. The beloved vicar was blackmailing; the bullying landlord was sheltering an addict daughter. Motive depth from moral complexity.
- **The Drawing-Room Reveal** — The Sleuth assembles the suspects (or the relevant subset) and names the killer. May happen at the church fete, the pub, the library, the cricket match. The form is non-negotiable; the venue varies.

**Supporting tropes (stack 3-6 per work):**
- **The Village Fete / Festival** — A community event provides the season's recurring backdrop; murders happen around it; suspects gather naturally.
- **The Disapproving Aunt** — A senior community member who reads the Sleuth as nosy; lands comic friction; sometimes provides the clinching observation by accident.
- **The Outsider's Eye** — The Sleuth is often slightly outside the community (incomer, vicar from a city parish, retiree from London, foreign DI) — sees what locals miss because they don't share the assumptions.
- **The Letter / Diary / Cassette** — A written or recorded document surfaces in the second act and reframes the Victim. Material evidence over witness testimony.
- **The Red Herring Suspect** — A loud, obvious suspect introduced early — too easy. The audience clocks them; the show clears them by the midpoint.
- **The Vicar's Tea** — Sleuth and Sidekick / Foil meet over tea / coffee / a pint in act two to lay out what they know. Audience catches up; suspect tree is pruned.
- **The Coincidence That Isn't** — Two unrelated village events (a fete cancellation, a delayed train, a power cut) turn out to be the Murderer's cover.
- **The Animal Witness** — A dog, a horse, a cat behaves anomalously in act one; the Sleuth notices later. Animals see what people perform around.
- **The Old Crime** — The current murder rhymes with an unsolved or covered-up crime from 20+ years ago. The village's past is the present's motive.
- **The Sleuth's Hobby** — A specific civilian skill (baking, gardening, knitting, antique restoration, baroque-music scholarship) provides at least one episode's clinching insight per season.
- **The Wedding / Funeral Disruption** — A ceremony is interrupted by a revelation. Communal ritual as suspense container.
- **The Innocent Suspect's Cost** — A wrongly suspected character loses something (job, marriage, reputation) before being cleared; the cost is real but recoverable.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't show gore. Bodies are discovered tidily; wounds are described, not displayed. Violence happens offscreen.
- Don't show sex on-screen. Affairs are central to motive; their depiction is implied, never explicit.
- Don't kill children or pets. (Adults only. The Animal Witness lives.)
- Don't break the community permanently. A murderer is removed; the village's social fabric — pub, church, post office, fete — survives intact. The next episode begins as if nothing happened.
- Don't make the Sleuth wrong about the killer. The Sleuth solves it. Genre contract.
- Don't make the killer a stranger. No drifters, no random violence. The closed circle is the floor.
- Don't withhold the Fair-Play clue. The audience must, in retrospect, be able to point to the moment they could have solved it.
- Don't introduce a serial killer. One murder per episode (occasionally two, linked). The genre is not about scale of evil; it's about restoration of order.

## Conformity rules
- The Sleuth solves the case. Always. Within the episode.
- The killer is inside the Closed Circle and is named by the Sleuth in a Drawing-Room Reveal.
- The Fair-Play Clue is shown to the audience before the reveal — in retrospect, the solution must have been visible.
- The village survives. The pub stays open, the vicar stays, the post office reopens by next Sunday.
- No gore, no explicit sex, no harmed children or pets. Hard floor.
- The Authority Foil and the Sleuth maintain their working partnership across the season — they may bicker, but they do not break.
- One named civilian competence in the Sleuth's hands is the show's signature. It must clinch at least one case per season.
- Tonal register may stretch to melancholy or chilly (Shetland, Vera), but the restorative ending is non-negotiable.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- The village's specific setting — English Cotswolds, Scottish island, Caribbean island, Cornish coast, New York apartment building (Only Murders), abbey, university town, Yorkshire moor.
- The Sleuth's civilian role — vicar, baker, retired schoolteacher, pathologist, podcaster, Detective Inspector who's eccentric enough to count, Justice of the Peace, librarian.
- Tonal register — sunlit-comic, gently-baroque, chilly-prestige, working-class-melancholic, faintly absurd.
- The Authority Foil's exact rank and disposition — village constable, visiting DI, retired chief inspector, the Sleuth's old friend from training.
- The closed-circle's mechanism — village isolation, building isolation, festival isolation, weather isolation, period isolation (pre-mobile-phone, pre-DNA).
- Period — Edwardian (Father Brown), 1950s (Sister Boniface), 1960s (Endeavour), contemporary, near-contemporary.
- Episode-of-the-week vs serialized — the modern slot allows light serialization (a personal throughline) but the cases must self-contain.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (community access is lost and restored; a premature accusation costs an innocent; the finale consumes fair-play clues and suspect choices) — every clue, secret, ritual, and relationship varies freely.

## Caveats
- Register stretches further than non-genre readers expect. Shetland and Vera are still cozies by recipe (closed circle, fair-play, Sleuth solves, community endures), but their tonal floor is much darker than Midsomer's. The Caveat is that "cozy" as a marketing word and the recipe as a structural object diverge — pitch the show by tone, derive it by structure.
- Common failure mode: a season-arc that escalates toward a serial killer or a conspiracy. This tips the show out of cozy and into thriller. The recipe holds the case-per-episode structure as the genre's contract; serialization is a thin throughline, not a season-long Big Bad.
- Common failure mode: gore creep — a writer's-room push toward "more impact" through more visible violence. The cozy recipe explicitly rejects this. Wounds described not shown; bodies tidy.
- Common failure mode: the Authority Foil written as antagonist. The Foil is the Sleuth's partner, not their obstacle. Friction is the relationship's texture, not its content.
- The Sleuth's specific hobby competence is often the single hardest element to commit to. Generic "she's observant" fails the recipe. Name the skill (baking, knitting, baroque-music research, antique restoration, podcasting) and let it solve at least one case per season.
- Genre-of-one risk: none. One of the most populated commercial slots in television.
