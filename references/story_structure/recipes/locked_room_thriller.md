# Locked Room Thriller

## Slot
A SMALL CAST is bound to a SINGLE LOCATION (mansion, island, ship, train, bunker, building, game, weather) by physical isolation, deceit, or contract; a DEATH or REVEAL forces them to investigate each other; the TRUTH emerges through proximity rather than evidence-gathering. Everyone is suspect; everyone is also victim; the room is the antagonist as much as any person inside it.

## Corpus
- *Knives Out* (Johnson)
- *Glass Onion* (Johnson)
- *Bodies Bodies Bodies* (Reijn)
- *Ready or Not* (Bettinelli-Olpin / Gillett)
- *Identity* (Mangold)
- *Clue* (Lynn)
- *The Hateful Eight* (Tarantino)
- *And Then There Were None* (Christie / multiple adaptations)
- *Murder on the Orient Express* (Christie / Branagh / Lumet)
- *Cube* (Natali)
- *The Menu* (Mylod)
- *Bullet Train* (Leitch)
- *The Lighthouse* (Eggers)
- *Saltburn* (final-act lock; partial fit)
- *Triangle of Sadness* (act two)

(Tonal range: Glass Onion's broad comedy through Knives Out's smart-comic mystery to The Hateful Eight's pressure-cooker western and The Lighthouse's existential horror. Same recipe — confinement + pressure + revelation — opposite registers.)

## Emotional contract
**Confinement + pressure + revelation.** The audience is locked in with the cast, watches social masks crack under proximity, and gets the pleasure of a truth that could only have surfaced because no one could leave.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Lock** — The physical or social mechanism that makes leaving impossible. Storm, island, contract, weather, terrain, a game with rules, a host's authority, a vehicle in motion. Named and shown in act one.
- **The Mask-Crack** — The moment a member of the In-Group's social performance fails under pressure. The defining beat of the genre; happens to most cast members across the runtime.
- **The False Confession / False Accusation** — A character either confesses to a thing they didn't do (panic, protection, performance) or accuses someone they know is innocent. The audience's read on the room is rewired.
- **The Reveal Set-Piece** — The act-three sequence where the truth is named — Blanc's drawing-room speech, the chef's menu, the host's monologue. Form is non-negotiable; staging varies.

**Supporting tropes (stack 3-6 per work):**
- **The Establishing Tour** — Act one moves through the location end-to-end so the audience maps the geography. Every space that pays off later is shown in this sequence.
- **The Inventory** — Weapons, exits, communications devices are catalogued. A wall display of knives, a satellite phone, a coded gate. What's available will be used.
- **The Ritual Meal** — The dinner / feast / opening drinks gathering where the In-Group's social grammar is on full display. Often the first death follows directly.
- **The Hidden Identity** — At least one character is not who they claim. The Outsider, the Help, or a new guest is something else (cop undercover, journalist, sister of an old victim, AI).
- **The In-Group Code** — A name, a phrase, a ritual that proves someone belongs (and outs the Outsider). The code is shown once and used as a clue later.
- **The Storm / Power Cut** — The Lock tightens — weather worsens, electricity dies, the road washes out, the staff leave. Resources contract on a visible schedule.
- **The Wrong Suspect Confrontation** — Mid-runtime, the In-Group corners a suspect (often the Outsider, the Help, or a guest perceived as low-status) who is innocent. The genre's class / prejudice argument lands here.
- **The Body Count Curve** — One death in act one, a second mid-act-two, optionally a third just before the reveal. Each death narrows the suspect pool; the curve is the show's tempo.
- **The Class Tell** — A small visible marker (shoes, vocabulary, table manners, a credit card declined, a brand misidentified) outs the Outsider or the secret-impostor. Often used twice — once to mark, once to invert.
- **The Trick of the Room** — The location has a secret feature — a hidden door, a panic room, an old dumbwaiter, a load-bearing structural lie — that the killer exploits. Shown early, paid off late.
- **The Truth-Speaker's Stunt** — The Detective figure performs the reveal theatrically — a chair turn, a slow walk, a recreated dinner. The audience's pleasure is the performance as much as the solution.
- **The Last Two** — Final act narrows to two characters in a confined sub-space (kitchen, attic, lifeboat, single corridor). The smaller room inside the locked room.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't let anyone leave halfway through. The Lock must hold from act one through the reveal. A character who escapes mid-runtime breaks the genre.
- Don't introduce a killer from outside the established cast. The audience must have met the killer in act one (even if disguised). New character at the reveal is a cheat.
- Don't kill the Outsider before the reveal. They are the audience's POV; their survival to the truth is part of the contract.
- Don't make the motive abstract or political. Motives are personal — money, infidelity, shame, an old wrong, a stolen credit. The genre is about people, not systems.
- Don't resolve the lock by deus ex — the storm doesn't conveniently pass, the rescue helicopter doesn't arrive on cue. The truth comes first; the door opens only after.
- Don't make all the In-Group equally guilty unless the genre is doing And Then There Were None / Orient Express explicitly (which is its own subvariant).
- Don't end with the killer victorious AND uncaught AND off-camera. They may escape with the truth, but the audience knows.

## Beat spine

(This recipe sizes to a film or a limited-series mini-arc — 8-10 beats. For a serialized 10-episode TV variant, each beat becomes an episode and the cast / location may rotate guests in and out within the Lock.)

| 3 | **The Lock Closes.** The worsening storm, departing staff, or expiring signal offers a last chance to end the event. Treating control as care, the Host refuses evacuation and activates the named lock; leaving becomes impossible on the Host's terms. **Needs:** E1-lock-control. **Sets up:** E3-sealed-lock (→E4, E6, E9, E10), E3-dead-signal (→E5). | Containment. The space tightens. |
| 4 | **The First Victim.** The Killer answers the private threat by using an inventoried tool and the tour's service latch, leaving a body whose position contradicts the public route. | 5 | **The Wrong Suspect.** Using the Outsider's code bluff and the dead signal as permission, the In-Group corners the easiest low-status suspect and confiscates their key or phone. | 6 | **The Second Death + the False Confession.** Because attention remains fixed on the wrong suspect, the Killer uses the same inventory gap to silence someone who can connect the route to them. | 7 | **The Trick of the Room.** Comparing both bodies with the Help's partial confession, the Outsider rejects the public route and tests the service latch shown on the tour. | 8 | **The Reveal Set-Piece.** The Truth-Speaker stages a public reconstruction from the complete guest list, hidden proof, missing tool, two bodies, Help's testimony, and the inverted code; the Killer and small personal motive are named. The survivors leave with the wrongful accusation visible between them; the Killer is dead, caught, or escaped with the truth known, and the location remains silent behind them. **Needs:** E1-lock-control, E3-sealed-lock, E5-wrong-accusation, E9-killer-capture, E9-broken-lock. **Sets up:** —. | Catharsis + faint unease. The room is empty; we know what happened inside it. |

## Conformity rules
- The Lock must be named, shown, and respected. No magical departures.
- The killer must be present from act one. The audience must, in retrospect, have had access to them.
- The motive must be personal and human. Money, infidelity, shame, an old betrayal, a stolen credit. Not "the system" and not psychopathy.
- The Outsider survives to the reveal (or to the Last Two). They are the audience's eyes.
- The Reveal Set-Piece is performed, not narrated in voiceover. The Truth-Speaker says it aloud in front of suspects.
- The Trick of the Room must be set up before it pays off. The geography is a clue.
- Comic and horror registers both fit — the recipe holds across Glass Onion and The Lighthouse — but the structural beats are identical.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- The Lock's specific mechanism — storm, island, vehicle, contract, game, sealed building, weather, social shame.
- The cast size and composition — three (Lighthouse) to twelve (Orient Express).
- Tonal register — broad comedy, smart-comic, pressure-cooker drama, gothic horror, existential horror.
- The Truth-Speaker's identity — formal detective, emergent survivor, the killer themselves (in some variants confessing).
- The class / prejudice argument's weight — overt (Knives Out, The Menu) to subtextual (Identity).
- The body count — one (Knives Out) to nearly the full cast (And Then There Were None).
- The location's specificity — high-design mansion, dive bar, fishing boat, mountain cabin, lighthouse, train carriage, party flat.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the Lock enables the deaths; the wrong accusation enables the second; the tour, testimony, and code solve the room) — every location, motive, tool, and class tell varies freely.

## Caveats
- The slot has a sub-variant where everyone is the killer (Orient Express, And Then There Were None's twists). This is a distinct recipe overlay — the In-Group conspires; the Outsider / Detective uncovers the conspiracy. Recipe holds with one inversion: motive becomes collective, but is still small and human per conspirator.
- Common failure mode: a script that lets a character leave for plot convenience (someone gets a phone signal, the road clears for one car). This breaks the Lock and erodes the genre's pressure. Hold the Lock or change recipes.
- Common failure mode: a killer revealed who was barely on screen. The reveal must invert the audience's read of someone they've been watching; a near-invisible killer feels like a cheat regardless of how well-clued.
- Common failure mode: the Outsider as savior. The Outsider's job is to see and to survive; they do not need to be combat-competent or romantically rewarded. Restraint at the Outsider's resolution keeps the genre honest.
- Register dial: the horror-leaning variant (Lighthouse, Ready or Not) skews the Mask-Crack into outright psychological collapse. The comic variant (Glass Onion) skews it into farce. Same beat, different decibel.
- Genre-of-one risk: none. The locked-room recipe is over a century old and adapts cleanly to new locations and technologies.
