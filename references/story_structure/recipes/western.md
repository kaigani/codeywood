# Western (Loneliness + Violence + Reckoning)

## Slot
The plain (non-supernatural) Western. A WIDE LANDSCAPE, a PROTAGONIST who carries (or is carried by) a CODE, and an UNAVOIDABLE CONFRONTATION with men whose code disagrees. Story turns on distance — between people, between law and justice, between who the protagonist was and who they will have to become to settle the matter. Land, weather, horses, weapons, and silences are the recipe's primary vocabulary. No curses, no Hellmouth, no supernatural element.

Same recipe — land, code, the reckoning that cannot be deferred.)

## Emotional contract
**Loneliness + violence + reckoning.** The audience watches a person on a wide land carry something — a debt, a vow, an old killing, a brother, a daughter — toward a meeting they cannot avoid, and pays the cost in the meeting. Catharsis is the price visible on the body and the land afterward. Even comic Westerns observe the cost. Even mythic Westerns observe the loneliness. The recipe will not deliver triumph clean.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Wide Shot** — Recurring image: figure(s) tiny against landscape. Genre's signature scale; reinforces the loneliness half of the cocktail. The recipe demands at least one per episode.
- **The Code** — The protagonist's stated or implicit rule — never shoot first, always finish what you started, family before law, honor the contract, get the man to Yuma. Spoken once, tested across the season; cost of keeping it is the recipe's spine.
- **The Ride** — Travel sequence — horses, wagons, trains, trucks (modern), days of geography crossed. The Western's primary narrative engine. The journey is not montage; it is the story.
- **The Confrontation** — Set-piece meeting between Protagonist and Antagonist — saloon, dusty street, ridge, ranch porch, jail, hotel room, river crossing. Spaced; deferred; choreographed in silences. The recipe will not skip it.
- **The Cost on the Body** — Violence registers physically — a wound that does not heal clean, a hand that no longer works, a partner buried, a daughter motherless, a brother hanged. The Reckoning is paid in flesh and dirt.

**Supporting tropes:**
- **The Saloon Scene** — Introduction or recurring waypoint — the cards, the piano, the women, the strangers eyeing the door. Western's social geometry compressed into one room.
- **The Wanted Poster / The Reward** — Bounty, debt, contract — economic motor for the Ride. Makes the Confrontation paid work even when it is personal.
- **The Mexican Standoff** — Three or more weapons drawn at once, time stops. Genre's signature suspense unit (Leone's grammar; recurs in Tarantino, Coens, Mangold).
- **The Old Killer Trying to Stay Out** — Protagonist has retired from violence — farming, raising daughters, hauling milk. The first ride drags them back. Recipe's most reliable hook.
- **The Town That Won't Stand With Him** — Townsfolk fail to back the Protagonist when the moment comes. High Noon's signature; Unforgiven inverts; Hell or High Water modernizes.
- **The Horse / The Truck** — Specific named vehicle — a horse with a name and history, a truck the Protagonist services, a wagon that breaks. Material specificity grounds the journey.
- **The Long Silence** — Two characters share screen time without speaking — fire, ride, meal, watch. The Western's emotional accounting medium.
- **The Bad Drink Scene** — Whiskey poured; either ritual companionship or its negation. The pour itself is the genre's social grammar.
- **The River Crossing / The Mountain Pass** — Natural threshold the party must cross; The Land asserts itself. Often the moment the journey changes character.
- **The Burial** — Shovel, dirt, a few words, no priest. Death registered in the recipe's physical vocabulary.
- **The Boy / The Apprentice** — Younger figure who watches the Protagonist work; learns the trade he was trying not to teach. (True Grit, Open Range, Power of the Dog's inverse). The next generation tested by the Confrontation.
- **The Repeated Threat** — Antagonist appears at distance early — a rider at the edge, a column of dust, a name on a poster — long before the Confrontation. Recipe's pulse beat.
- **The Last Stand / The Walk** — Final approach to the Confrontation: a slow walk down a street, a ride into the canyon, a stand in the doorway. Genre's spine moment.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't supply supernatural elements. Curses, ghosts, hellmouths, prophetic dreams — wrong recipe. (See cursed_gunslinger_western for that variant.) The Western proper holds at the natural register.
- Don't let the Protagonist win cleanly. Even when they survive, the cost is on the body, the land, or the family. Unblemished triumph is the wrong genre.
- Don't make the Antagonist cartoon. Their code must be coherent — even Anton Chigurh's coin is a code. Comic mustache-twirling breaks the symmetry.
- Don't let the town's cowardice be punished. High Noon's bitter ending is recipe-correct; townsfolk failure must register without revenge against them.
- Don't skip the Ride. Helicoptering the Protagonist to the Confrontation breaks the recipe. The geography crossed is the cost accruing.
- Don't deliver the Confrontation in a single quick exchange without staging. Wide shots, silences, footsteps, the wind — the meeting is the recipe's most slowed scene, not its fastest.
- Don't moralize the violence with voiceover. The recipe shows; it does not explain. The land and the bodies make the argument.
- Don't introduce a chosen-one mechanic. The Protagonist is competent because of trade and survival; never because of prophecy or destiny.

| Episode | Beat | Emotional target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Land + The Code.** Wide country and a smaller present life establish what the Protagonist has shrunk down to after earlier violence. When the One Who Asks arrives with a debt only a rider can collect, the Protagonist chooses a private vow over the courthouse and digs up the rifle that choice requires. **Needs:** —. **Sets up:** E1-spoken-vow (→E2, E9), E1-buried-rifle (→E10). | Stillness laced with summons. The audience feels the size of the land and the weight on the shoulders. |
| 2 | **The Ride Begins.** Bound by the vow, the Protagonist takes the road they were trying to avoid and accepts a Partner because the distance is too large for one body to cross alone. A warning left on a fence, corpse, or way-station proves the Antagonist already knows someone is coming, and the route chosen here fixes where the party can later cross. **Needs:** E1-spoken-vow. **Sets up:** E2-rider-warning (→E3, E8), E2-trail-map (→E6). | Companionship + foreboding. The audience commits to the journey. |
| 3 | **The Saloon / The First Town.** The first town gives them news at a price: by refusing an easy shooting in public, the Protagonist earns a reputation for rules and leaves behind a gunman who can still carry tales. The Antagonist answers that restraint with a message, turning the saloon's temporary peace into proof that the conflict is now visible to everybody. **Needs:** E2-rider-warning. **Sets up:** E3-saloon-reputation (→E4, E7), E3-spared-gunman (→E8, E10). | Genre pleasure (saloon grammar) + sharpened stakes. |
| 4 | **The Lawman Enters.** The Lawman arrives once the saloon talk has a name on it, and the state's version of justice finally catches up with the private one already underway. To keep the rider moving, the One Who Asks conceals the paper or testimony that would have handed the matter back to procedure, while the badge names what the Protagonist will be charged with if they continue. **Needs:** E3-saloon-reputation. **Sets up:** E4-withheld-affidavit (→E5), E4-rival-badge (→E5, E10). | Pressure. The audience reads the law's geometry; the Protagonist's path narrows. |
| 5 | **The First Blood.** The concealed paper leaves nobody with a clean legal exit, so the first clash happens in the open and somebody dies under the Protagonist's protection rather than under arrest. When the Partner chases a fleeing scout instead of holding position, the party wins the fight but loses a horse and digs a grave that makes the journey physically poorer. **Needs:** E4-withheld-affidavit, E4-rival-badge. **Sets up:** E5-lost-horse (→E6), E5-fresh-grave (→E9, E10). | Awe + revulsion. The recipe insists on the cost of violence even when justified. |
| 6 | **The River Crossing / The Mountain Pass.** The route chosen in E2 now becomes a trap because the lost horse forces the party onto the only ford or pass they can still make before the Antagonist closes. The Lawman, trusting procedure more than people, sends a telegraphed warning ahead and strips the party of cartridges or lawful shelter, so surviving the crossing leaves them alive but publicly outnumbered. **Needs:** E2-trail-map, E5-lost-horse. **Sets up:** E6-telegraph-warning (→E7, E10), E6-empty-bandolier (→E8, E10). | Vertigo of distance. | 7 | **The Town That Won't Stand With Him.** Because the warning reached town before the riders did, every porch reads the Protagonist through fear and paperwork rather than need. | 8 | **The Antagonist Named.** The betrayal in E7 puts the meeting exactly where the Antagonist wanted it, and the spared gunman from the saloon can now identify every rider in the party. Across a street, porch, fire, or cut in the hills, the Antagonist names his code against the Protagonist's while storm, darkness, or bad ground closes off retreat and leaves the party with too little ammunition for a second try. **Needs:** E2-rider-warning, E3-spared-gunman, E6-empty-bandolier, E7-sold-room. **Sets up:** E8-storm-cutoff (→E9), E8-broken-truce (→E10). | Recognition + cold dread. | 9 | **The Cost Lands.** The vow from E1, the grave from E5, and the shut doors from E7 leave the Protagonist nowhere to hand the burden but forward, so the cost falls on the nearest human bond when the storm cutoff delivers someone close into the Antagonist's reach. ## Conformity rules
- The geography must be specific, vast, and present in every episode. The Wide Shot is non-negotiable. Indoor-only Westerns are the wrong recipe.
- The Code must be stated or staged early and tested under pressure. A Protagonist without a code is a thriller protagonist; the Western requires the spine.
- Violence must cost. Cuts that heal cleanly, clean conscience, easy regret — the recipe rejects all three. The Cost on the Body / Land / Family is the genre's morality.
- The Antagonist must be morally legible. Not sympathetic necessarily — but their grievance, appetite, or code must be coherent enough that the Confrontation is between two arguments.
- The Ride must occupy real screen time. Travel is the story; collapsing it is the wrong genre.
- The Confrontation must be staged at the recipe's full slow grammar — silences, footsteps, distance, draw. Quick action-movie pacing breaks the Western register.
- No supernatural mechanics. Curses, ghosts, dreams-as-prophecy, the Devil, undead — all belong to the supernatural Western (cursed_gunslinger_western), not here.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Era — classical (1860s-1890s), turn-of-century (Yuma, Open Range), Mexican Revolution (Wild Bunch adjacents), Depression-era (Power of the Dog set 1925), neo-Western contemporary (Hell or High Water, Yellowstone, No Country, Sisters Brothers adjacent). Recipe scales across.
- Setting — desert, plains, mountain, snow, river country, ranch country, oil country (modern). Land's signature is dial.
- Vehicle — horse, wagon, train, foot, truck, helicopter (modern). The Ride survives the upgrade.
- Lawman position — Protagonist (Tombstone, High Noon), antagonist (Django, Unforgiven), absent (Open Range, Power of the Dog), allied late (3:10, Hell or High Water). The badge's politics is dial.
- Tonal register — classical-mythic (Magnificent Seven, Open Range), revisionist-bleak (Unforgiven, McCabe, Power of the Dog), elegiac-modern (No Country, Hell or High Water), prestige-profane (Deadwood), pulp-baroque (Django, Tarantino adjacents), chamber-quiet (First Cow, Sisters Brothers, Power of the Dog), Leone-operatic. Recipe handles all.
- Whether the Confrontation kills the Antagonist, the Protagonist, both, or neither. All four valid; pick for the recipe's last verdict.
- Whether the Protagonist's Code holds, breaks, or transmutes. Three different Westerns from the same setup.
- Race / class / nation — Black gunfighter (Django, Lone Hand), Chinese trader (First Cow), Mexican-American family (Open Range adjacent), Indigenous resistance (Killers of the Flower Moon adjacent). The recipe's vehicle expands; the engine stays.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the ride consumes the vow; the town's betrayal consumes the law's warning; the last walk consumes the vow, the grave, and the broken truce) — the CONTENT of every edge varies freely: whose code, what debt, which terrain, what body pays.

## Caveats
- Register dial: comic Westerns (Cat Ballou, Sisters Brothers' lean, Hell or High Water's wit) keep the recipe if they honor the cost. Pure comedy without reckoning drifts to comic adventure.
- Genre proximity: the supernatural Western (cursed_gunslinger_western) is the recipe with a Hellmouth and a curse layered in — same loneliness, code, and reckoning, but the violence reaches into the inhuman. If your draft introduces ghosts, prophecies, or undead, switch recipes. This file is the plain register only.
- Genre proximity: modern crime-Westerns (No Country, Hell or High Water, Yellowstone) are this recipe at the contemporary register. If the land, the code, and the reckoning are all present, even pickup trucks and oil rigs are recipe-compatible.
- Common failure mode: the Code unspecified. If the audience cannot name the Protagonist's rule by Episode 2, the season's spine is bent. Stage the code early.
- Common failure mode: tourist-Western. Outsider perspectives that treat the land as exotic without engaging the Ride's labor read as costume drama. The Land must work the Protagonist, not just decorate them.
- Common failure mode: thriller pacing imposed on Western beats. Quick cuts, score-pumping, jump-scare Confrontations — the recipe collapses if its silences are not honored. Even Tarantino's pulp Westerns dwell.
- Common failure mode: the Antagonist as evil cipher. Their argument matters. Anton Chigurh, Frank (OUATITW), Daggett (Unforgiven), the Toby brothers (Hell or High Water) all have philosophies. Cartoon villains break the recipe.
- Common failure mode: the moral easy-out. If the Protagonist's violence comes without cost — wounds heal, sidekick survives, town thanks them — the Reckoning half of the cocktail has not run.
