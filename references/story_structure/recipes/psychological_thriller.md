# Psychological Thriller (Doubt + Paranoia + Collapse)

## Slot
Serialized fiction where a protagonist's perception of their own reality — what they remember, what they did, who they love, who they are — is the battlefield. The threat may be external (stalker, conspiracy, gaslighting partner, killer in the next room) or internal (dissociation, repression, unreliable memory, a self that has gone elsewhere). The genre's engine is the protagonist's MIND under pressure. Plot serves doubt; doubt serves collapse. The audience watches certainty erode until something gives.

(Tonal range: from Fatal Attraction / Fight Club's pulp-Hitchcockian and You's CW-camp through Sharp Objects / Big Little Lies's prestige-mainstream and Gone Girl / Mr. Robot's prestige-mid-tier to Black Swan / Severance / Shutter Island's prestige-A24 / formalist-experiment. Same recipe — perception under pressure until something gives.)

## Emotional contract
**Doubt + paranoia + collapse.** Audience starts inside the protagonist's perspective and trusts it. Doubt is seeded early — a detail wrong, a memory off, a witness contradicting, a face seen twice. Paranoia is the steady accumulation — every relationship suspect, every room compromised, every act of recall a small terror. Collapse is the cocktail's release: a moment where the protagonist's self / story / world fractures, sometimes catastrophically, sometimes quietly. The audience pays in destabilization that compounds.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Trusted POV** — Pilot establishes the protagonist as our reliable narrator — voiceover, point-of-view shots, interiority, articulate self-account. The audience signs the contract: we will see through these eyes. The contract will be broken.
- **The First Wrong Detail** — Episode 1 or 2 plants a small inconsistency — a memory off by an hour, a name remembered wrong, a door that should have been locked, a face seen twice. The seed of all later doubt.
- **The Gaslight Beat** — A scene where someone close to the protagonist contradicts their account — "you said that yourself," "you were there," "you imagined it" — and the protagonist starts to question. Required at least twice per season.
- **The Collapse** — A moment late in the season where the protagonist's worldview, identity, or memory FRACTURES — twist revealed, dissociated act exposed, identity collapsed into the Echo, the killer named as themselves, the spouse confirmed as the adversary, the institution exposed. The cocktail's release.
- **The After-Image** — The closing register — the protagonist alive but altered; the audience leaves with the residue of what cannot be put back.

**Supporting tropes:**
- **The Lost Hour / Lost Day** — A gap in the protagonist's memory or timeline — an evening they can't account for, a journal entry they don't remember writing, a credit-card charge in a city they didn't visit. The audience clocks it before the protagonist does.
- **The Unreliable Reflection** — Mirrors, screens, photographs, video — the protagonist sees themselves and the image is wrong (Black Swan, Severance, Fight Club). Visual paranoia.
- **The Confidant's Doubt** — Mid-season, the trusted listener starts to question the protagonist's account out loud. Often the protagonist's last bridge to consensus reality.
- **The Found Document** — Journal, recording, email, video, security footage that either confirms or contradicts the protagonist's memory. Usually does both — solves one question and opens a worse one.
- **The Recurring Witness** — A bystander, neighbor, child, or stranger who keeps appearing where they shouldn't. The audience doesn't know whether the protagonist is being followed or assembling a pattern out of nothing.
- **The Domestic Strain** — Marriage / family / friendship is corroded by the protagonist's secret-keeping, paranoia, or behavior. The Stake fades.
- **The Therapist Scene** — A formal session where the protagonist is in dialogue with a professional listener; the doubt is articulated in clinical language. Often plants the diagnostic the audience will use later.
- **The Stalker Geography** — The Adversary's intrusion on space — the apartment, the car, the office, the route home; visible signs (rearranged objects, faint surveillance) that the protagonist may or may not be seeing.
- **The False Comfort** — A scene where the protagonist relaxes, takes a bath, sleeps deeply, accepts a drink — the audience tightens because the genre punishes ease.
- **The Big Reveal** — Late-stage twist: the Adversary is the Confidant; the protagonist did the killing; the Echo was always them; the institution was sponsoring the abuse; the spouse was the planner. Earned through planting, not surprise.
- **The Body Memory** — Physical symptom that knows what the protagonist hasn't admitted — bruise they can't explain, scar that wasn't there, blood on the cuff, the wrong key in the pocket. The recipe's most reliable instrument.
- **The Public Performance** — Protagonist must pass as fine — dinner, gala, interview, sermon, presentation, family event — while internally collapsing. Genre's grace-under-erosion set piece.
- **The Domestic Object Wrong** — A small thing in the home is off — child's drawing, vase moved, drawer not where it was left, an extra plate. The recipe's quiet anchor for paranoia.
- **The Witness Recanted** — A character who could have corroborated the protagonist's account either lies, dies, or vanishes. The collapse's plumbing.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't make the threat clearly supernatural without committing — psychological thriller's adversary is a person, a system, or the protagonist's mind. If the answer is ghosts, the slot is supernatural horror.
- Don't externalize the source too early. The genre wants doubt; if the audience knows by Episode 3 that the spouse is the killer, the genre slides to thriller-suspense.
- Don't let the protagonist's mental-health symptom be a cosmetic affectation. The flaw must be specific — repression, dissociation, alcoholism, mania, denial — and must generate the doubt, not just decorate it.
- Don't trust the protagonist's account at face value AND don't make them a flat liar. The mid-genre register — protagonist who believes themselves but is wrong about something specific — is where the recipe lives.
- Don't reset the collapse. If the protagonist's worldview shatters and then is restored by Episode 10 with no residue, the contract is broken. The After-Image is required.
- Don't make the Adversary a slasher. They must read as articulate, charming, plausible, often the kind of person no one would suspect. Predation here is intimate.
- Don't explain the protagonist's psychology in expository dialogue. Show through behavior, body memory, Found Document, Therapist Scene. Lecture kills the genre's instability.
- Don't kill the Stake without registering the cost. Domestic Strain that ends in the spouse / child / friend leaving must land — the audience has been paying attention to that thread.

## Conformity rules
- The protagonist must START as a reliable POV. The contract is the trust the genre will break. If the audience knows the protagonist is unreliable in Episode 1, the recipe has slid to character study.
- The cognitive flaw must be SPECIFIC and named — dissociation, repression, alcoholism, mania, traumatic amnesia, obsession, denial. Generic "instability" is descriptive and useless.
- The first wrong detail must land in Episode 1 or 2. The seed of doubt is non-negotiable.
- Body Memory must register at least twice — the body knows what the mind doesn't. Bruise, scar, blood, taste, smell, posture.
- The Adversary, if external, must be a SPECIFIC named person with a worldview — intimate, articulate, plausible. Faceless threats belong to conspiracy thriller.
- The Collapse must land — the protagonist's worldview fractures by Episode 10. Restoration / reset breaks the contract.
- The After-Image must register — the protagonist is altered. The closing shot must let the audience feel the residue.
- Reality-check characters (Confidant, Witness) must exist on screen and must doubt the protagonist at some point. Without external check, the genre slides to interior monologue.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Tonal register — pulp-Hitchcockian (Fatal Attraction, Sixth Sense pulpy register, You), prestige-mainstream (Big Little Lies, Sharp Objects, Gone Girl), formalist-experiment (Memento, Fight Club, Severance), A24-art-house (Black Swan, Shutter Island).
- The Adversary's substrate — intimate partner / gaslighter (Gone Girl, You, Fatal Attraction, Big Little Lies), stalker / predator (Cape Fear-adjacent, You's lean), institutional (Severance, Mr. Robot, Shutter Island), the protagonist themselves (Fight Club, Sixth Sense, Memento, Black Swan, Shutter Island).
- The protagonist's cognitive substrate — dissociation (Fight Club, Severance), repression (Sharp Objects, Shutter Island), alcoholism / blackout (Girl on the Train), obsession (Mr. Ripley, Black Swan), mania / OCD (Mr. Robot, Black Swan), unreliable narration around grief (Sixth Sense, Shutter Island).
- The protagonist's profession — analyst, dancer, journalist, parent, hacker, writer, novelist, office worker, detective, doctor. Each register dial.
- The setting compression — single household (Big Little Lies, Sharp Objects), single workplace (Severance, Mr. Robot), single train ride (Girl on the Train), single asylum / island (Shutter Island, Black Swan late), urban-spread (You, Gone Girl).
- Whether the reveal is twist (Sixth Sense, Fight Club, Shutter Island) or accumulated (Gone Girl, Mr. Robot, Big Little Lies). Both conform.
- Whether the protagonist survives intact, is incarcerated, is dead, or is permanently altered — register dial.
- POV games — voiceover (Fight Club, Mr. Robot), interior monologue, diary, second-person (You), procedural-reverse (Memento). Each a register dial.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (concealment enables the gaslight; a bounded record deepens isolation; the Collapse consumes choices about trust and corroboration) — every symptom, document, intimate threat, and final reading varies freely.

## Caveats
- Common failure mode: descriptive psychology. If the protagonist's flaw is "anxiety" or "trauma" with no specific named mechanism, the doubt cannot be engineered. Dissociation generates Lost Hours; alcoholism generates blackouts; repression generates Body Memory. Name the engine.
- Common failure mode: ghosts. The genre tolerates supernatural readings (Sixth Sense is the canonical) but only when the formal collapse confirms a psychological reading underneath. If the show commits to ghosts as ghosts, the slot is supernatural horror.
- Common failure mode: clean restoration. If the Stake is recovered with no residue, the After-Image fails, and the audience leaves with no destabilization. Even Hitchcockian-pulp endings (Fatal Attraction) leave the family altered.
- Common failure mode: the Confidant who is purely useful. If the trusted listener never doubts the protagonist, the recipe has no reality-check vector and slides to interior monologue.
- Common failure mode: the Adversary as slasher. If the threat is a hooded killer with no interiority, the genre is slasher-pulp or suspense, not psychological thriller. The Adversary must be intimate — gaslight, possession, predation with charm, institutional design.
- Register dial: reveal timing. Twist-late (Sixth Sense, Shutter Island) holds the collapse for Episode 10; accumulated (Mr. Robot, Big Little Lies, Sharp Objects) leaks the truth across episodes 6-9. Both conform; declare the strategy at intake.
- Register dial: protagonist culpability. The protagonist may be victim (Sharp Objects, Big Little Lies), perpetrator (Gone Girl second half, You, Ripley), or both (Fight Club, Severance, Black Swan). All conform; the genre's discomfort is the protagonist's morality being unstable along with their perception.
- Genre proximity: thriller-suspense puts the threat outside the protagonist's mind and lets the audience know it; psychological thriller keeps the threat ambiguous until late. If the audience knows the killer in Episode 2 and watches the protagonist not-know, the slot is suspense, not psychological thriller.
