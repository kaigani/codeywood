# Post-Apocalyptic (Loss + Endurance + Renewal)

## Slot
Serialized fiction set after the collapse — plague, war, climate, infection, nuclear, ecological — in which survivors carry the world they lost on their backs and try to make a new one out of what remains. The protagonist is a body that did not die when it should have. The genre is the long argument between despair and the small act of building. The road, the camp, the ruined city, the silent forest — geography is character.

(Tonal range: from Mad Max's pulp-kinetic and The 100's YA-action through The Walking Dead and The Last of Us's prestige-mainstream to McCarthy / Cuarón's literary-elegiac and Station Eleven's prestige-meditative. Same recipe — survivor carrying the lost world, finding the choice to keep making.)

## Emotional contract
**Loss + endurance + renewal.** Audience opens in the aftermath — silence, ruin, ash, weather, the small sounds of an emptied world. Endurance is the daily protagonist verb — water, food, shelter, fire, the next mile. Renewal is the cocktail's payoff: not restoration, not rebuilding the lost world, but the seeding of something new — a child, a garden, a song, a road, a town with a name. The cocktail is the ache of what was and the small permission to keep going.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Aftermath Image** — Pilot opens with a wide shot of the world after — silent highway, overgrown city, empty stadium, snow-deep house, ocean of cars. Worldbuilding as silence. The audience smells the loss before the dialogue.
- **The Daily Verb** — Survival as a series of small repeated acts: boil water, find batteries, set traps, walk miles, count rounds, dig latrines, ration food, mend boots. The genre's pulse.
- **The Charge Acquired** — Inciting moment when the Carrier is given (or takes) responsibility for the Charge. The job becomes the season.
- **The Settlement Offered** — Midpoint when the Carrier finds (or is offered) a place where building is happening. The protagonist's first real temptation off the road.
- **The Renewal Act** — Climax delivers a moment of making — a child born, a crop planted, a town named, a song sung, a road opened, a book passed on. Small, specific, costly. The cocktail's release.

**Supporting tropes:**
- **The Found Document** — Letter, log, recording, journal from someone who was alive during or just after the collapse. Often gives the Carrier their next move or seals their grief.
- **The Quiet Forage Scene** — A scavenging sequence in a familiar mundane place rendered eerie — supermarket, school, suburban kitchen, library, drugstore. Genre's emotional currency for the lost normal.
- **The Road Trade** — Two parties meet on the road, weapons visible, negotiate over goods / information / passage. Suspicion as procedure.
- **The Settlement Tour** — When a settlement is introduced, a Settler shows the Carrier around — the well, the wall, the garden, the schoolhouse, the medic's tent. The audience falls in love with the place that will be threatened.
- **The Infected Run / Raider Raid** — A set-piece assault on a settlement, camp, or road group. The cost-event of the season's act 2 or 3.
- **The Old World Object** — A surviving artifact from before — a working phone, a recording, a book, a costume, a working car, a child's toy. Sometimes powers a Wonder Beat; sometimes triggers the Lost.
- **The Camp at Night** — Fire, watch, conversation. The genre's intimacy — small lives by small light in a large dark. Required.
- **The Mercy Killing** — A character is bitten / infected / dying past saving, and someone in the Crew does the necessary thing. The recipe's hardest beat.
- **The Map and the Mile** — Physical geography (map, route, distance) is shown and felt. The journey is in miles, not abstractions.
- **The Pregnancy / Child / Birth** — A baby, a kid, or a pregnancy appears and becomes the genre's literal renewal. Sometimes the Charge; sometimes a later arrival.
- **The Old Skill Re-emerged** — Carrier or Settler demonstrates a pre-collapse skill — engineering, midwifery, broadcasting, agriculture, smithing, medicine — and the world remembers it had use for them.
- **The Settlement Falls** — A built place is destroyed (burned, infected, taken, abandoned). The genre charges the audience for hope.
- **The Last Radio Voice** — A transmission from elsewhere — another colony, an old broadcast, a CB call. Tells the Carrier the world isn't only what they can see.
- **The Refusal to Remain Human** — A character makes the choice to give up on the renewal contract — joins a Raider band, eats what shouldn't be eaten, kills the wrong body, leaves the Charge. The genre's variation lane.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't restore the lost world. The genre's argument is renewal, not restoration. The grid does not come back; the cities are not repopulated; the cure does not undo the world. New is the recipe.
- Don't make the Raider / Warlord stupid. The antagonist must read as a coherent worldview — predation has a logic in this world. If the antagonist is cackling, the genre slides into pulp action.
- Don't let the Charge die in a way that erases the renewal. The Charge is the recipe's hostage; if they die meaninglessly, the contract is broken. They may pay partially.
- Don't end without a making act — child, crop, song, town, road, book. Renewal must be SPECIFIC and visible. If the protagonist just keeps walking, the contract is broken (literary variation excepted; see Caveats).
- Don't reveal the cause of collapse in late-stage exposition. The cause is environment / behavior / found document — never a Wikipedia dump.
- Don't make the Settler a fool. The settled community's worldview must be coherent and dignified. If the genre treats building as naïveté, the renewal cocktail evaporates.
- Don't make the road itself easy. The Daily Verb must register. If shelter, food, water, miles are free, the genre is the wrong recipe.

## Conformity rules
- The collapse must be the ENVIRONMENT, not the plot. By Episode 3, the rules of the world (what kills, what feeds, what moves) must be visible through behavior. No exposition dump on the cause.
- The Daily Verb must register — water, food, fire, miles, rounds, hours of light, watch rotation. The audience must FEEL the labor of survival.
- The Charge must be specific — a named body, package, or seed. Without a Charge the protagonist is just wandering; the recipe is wrong.
- The Settler must exist on screen by Episode 3-4. The genre's argument is renewal vs predation, and renewal needs a face.
- The Antagonist must have a worldview — predation as law, ideology, hunger, infection-logic, religion. Not cartoon, not cackling.
- The climax must contain a MAKING ACT — child, crop, song, town, road, book, broadcast, name. Specific and visible. Variation lane exists (see refusal variant) but the recipe FLOOR is renewal.
- The Ferryman / Memory must give and pay. Pre-collapse knowledge cannot survive cost-free.
- The Lost must be integrated, not erased. The protagonist's frozen grief must thaw and become part of the renewal — not forgotten, not deleted.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Tonal register — pulp-kinetic (Mad Max, Fallout, The 100), prestige-mainstream (Walking Dead, Last of Us, Sweet Tooth), prestige-meditative (Station Eleven, Children of Men), literary-elegiac (The Road, McCarthy-adjacent).
- The collapse's substrate — plague (Last of Us, Station Eleven), war (Mad Max, Fallout), climate (The Road implicitly), infection-zombie (Walking Dead, I Am Legend), fertility / silence (Children of Men, A Quiet Place), nuclear (Fallout, BSG-lean).
- The Charge's substrate — child (Last of Us, Children of Men, Mad Max, Sweet Tooth), immune body (Last of Us, I Am Legend), seed / cure (Book of Eli, Snowpiercer), troupe / culture (Station Eleven), pregnancy (Children of Men, BSG).
- Setting — wasteland desert (Mad Max), forest-rural (Last of Us, Walking Dead, Sweet Tooth), road-monolithic (The Road), urban-ruin (I Am Legend, Children of Men), ship (BSG), train (Snowpiercer).
- Settlement design — agrarian (Walking Dead's Alexandria), pre-industrial (Last of Us's Jackson), theocratic (Snowpiercer), troupe-mobile (Station Eleven), city-fortress (BSG), tribal-coastal (Apes).
- Whether the protagonist accepts settlement or stays on the road — register dial. Last of Us's Joel partial-settles; Mad Max's Max never does; Station Eleven's troupe settles into motion. All conform.
- Whether the world's silence is acoustic (Quiet Place), visual (The Road's gray), ecological (Children of Men), or political (Snowpiercer). Each register dial.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the Charge's compassion creates exposure; settlement openness creates both vulnerability and the renewal method; grief, spent resources, and inherited knowledge determine the recovery) — every road, resource, loss, and making act varies freely.

## Caveats
- Critical register dial: RENEWAL vs REFUSAL. The recipe's contract is renewal — child, crop, song, town, road, book. McCarthy's *The Road* threads the harder lane: the renewal is the choice to remain human (carry the fire, refuse to eat the wrong meat, hand the boy to better people at the end). Treat renewal as the recipe's FLOOR and the choice-to-remain-human as a literary-variation lane. If a project lands fully in that lane, name it deliberately in the Variation rules — the audience contract changes.
- Common failure mode: the apocalypse as costume. If the show is a road-trip-with-monsters that could be set anywhere, the recipe collapses. The collapse must be felt — silence, geography, ration, weather, daily verbs.
- Common failure mode: restoration. If by season's end the grid is humming, the cure is shipping, and the highways are open — the recipe is the wrong genre (that's recovery sci-fi). The world stays broken; the protagonist makes something new inside it.
- Common failure mode: cartoon raiders. Negan-as-bonk, helmet-with-spikes-and-no-philosophy raiders read as pulp action. The Walking Dead's later seasons learned this. The Warlord must have a worldview.
- Common failure mode: the Charge mishandled. If the Charge is a McGuffin (a glowing case), the renewal cocktail collapses. The Charge must be specific, named, bonded to the Carrier.
- Genre proximity: dystopian shares ruin imagery but has an engineered regime; post-apocalyptic has SURVIVAL as the engine and no working regime. If there's a coherent state broadcasting ceremonies, the slot is dystopian, not post-apocalyptic.
- Register dial: zombie-pulp (Walking Dead seasons 1-3, World War Z), survival-prestige (Last of Us, Walking Dead late, Station Eleven), literary-elegiac (The Road, Children of Men). The 10-episode spine works for all three — adjust set-piece density and silence ratio.
