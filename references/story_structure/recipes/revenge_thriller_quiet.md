# Revenge Thriller (Quiet)

## Slot
A controlled, slow-burning revenge narrative where a CALM PROTAGONIST with a specific old wound TARGETS an identified person or small structure with patience, low body count, and high personal cost. Violence is sparse; restraint is the show's discipline; the protagonist's interior is the engine. Not action-movie revenge. Not power fantasy.

## Corpus
- *You Were Never Really Here* (Ramsay)
- *The Limey* (Soderbergh)
- *Blue Ruin* (Saulnier)
- *Cold in July* (Mickle)
- *Promising Young Woman* (Fennell)
- *Hold the Dark* (Saulnier)
- *The Killer* (Fincher)
- *Killing Eve* (BBC America — Villanelle's tracks fit)
- *Hannibal* (Fuller / NBC)
- *Three Billboards Outside Ebbing, Missouri* (McDonagh)
- *Mare of Easttown* (HBO — Mare's revenge sub-arc fits)
- *The Beguiled* (Coppola)
- *Prisoners* (Villeneuve)
- *Wind River* (Sheridan)

(Tonal range: Blue Ruin's wordless-vérité through The Limey's elliptical-melancholic to Promising Young Woman's chilly-prestige and Fincher's The Killer's near-comic-procedural. Same recipe — patience + targeting + reckoning — opposite registers.)

## Emotional contract
**Patience + targeting + reckoning.** The audience watches a wounded person carry out a plan with quiet competence, dreads what it will cost them more than what it will cost the target, and feels the bleak relief of a price paid in full.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Wound** — A specific past event (a child killed, a sister assaulted, a friend's overdose covered up, an institutional betrayal) shown in fragmented flashback or implied through behavior. Named, specific, unhealed.
- **The Plan** — A method of finding and reaching the Target — competent, patient, methodical. The audience watches the Plan execute and trusts the Avenger's professionalism.
- **The Cost Curve** — Each step toward the Target costs the Avenger something — a relationship, a body part, a piece of identity, the Tether. The curve is visible; it ends near zero.
- **The Reckoning** — The face-to-face confrontation with the Target. Not a chase; a meeting. Quiet, slow, possibly anti-climactic by action-movie standards. The genre's pleasure is the meeting, not the kill.

**Supporting tropes (stack 3-6 per work):**
- **The Tool** — The Avenger has a specific weapon, vehicle, or technique that becomes signature — a hammer, a particular gun, a syringe, a knife, a recording device. Material and overused; the audience reads it.
- **The Ritual** — A repeated action the Avenger performs (the hammer wrapped, the playlist, the daily run, the precise breakfast) that the show returns to. Discipline as costume; possibly OCD; possibly survival.
- **The Trigger** — A specific stimulus (a song, a photograph, a place, a name) that breaks the Avenger's calm momentarily. Shown 2-3 times across the runtime.
- **The Sympathetic Cop** — A police officer who is closing in, understands what the Avenger is doing, and is in friction with their own duty. May choose to look the other way.
- **The Almost-Pull-Back** — Mid-runtime, the Avenger has the chance to stop — the Tether asks, the Plan goes wrong, the Echo Victim is rescued. They don't. The choice is the show's thesis.
- **The Misread Civilian** — A bystander the Avenger nearly hurts because they were misread as a threat (or as the Target). The show's reminder that the Avenger is no longer reliable.
- **The Quiet Kill** — When violence happens it's brief, ugly, and unscored. No spectacle. No quip. The camera holds on the aftermath.
- **The Long Wait** — A sequence of pure stillness — sitting in a car, watching a house, waiting for a door to open. The genre's rhythm; the audience learns to read the protagonist's interior in stillness.
- **The Old Photograph** — A material object representing the Wound, carried, looked at, sometimes destroyed. Sometimes the only image of the lost person the audience sees.
- **The Handler's Last Call** — A scene with the Handler / Old Life figure where the prior life is acknowledged and refused. Often before the Reckoning.
- **The Mirror Victim** — A scene where the Avenger encounters someone in their own old role — wounded, beginning a revenge, considering it. The Avenger either helps or warns.
- **The Quiet Domesticity** — A scene of the Avenger doing something mundane (cooking, gardening, taking a bath) shot with attention. The genre's argument that this person was once whole; they may not be whole again.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't write set-piece action. No martial arts choreography, no car chases shot for spectacle, no extended shootouts. When violence happens, it's brief and ugly.
- Don't quip. The Avenger does not have one-liners. Comedy is rare and always uncomfortable.
- Don't redeem the Target with a sympathy speech. They may explain themselves; they do not earn pity.
- Don't heal the Avenger through the Reckoning. The wound is not cured by the kill. Most quiet-revenge narratives end with the Avenger more diminished than they began.
- Don't kill the Target offscreen. The audience must witness the meeting. The Reckoning is the genre's contract.
- Don't make the Avenger's competence supernatural or implausibly skilled. They are dangerous because they are patient and prepared, not because they are exceptional.
- Don't end with the Avenger riding off into a sequel-shaped future. The genre's tonal floor is bleak. Open endings work; victorious ones break the recipe.
- Don't introduce a love interest who heals the Avenger. The Tether is not a romance plot.

## Conformity rules
- The Avenger must be calm. Not stoic-action-hero calm — frozen-by-grief calm. The interior temperature is the show's signature.
- Violence is sparse and ugly. The Reckoning may be the only on-screen killing. No set-pieces.
- The Wound must be specific. A named loss, a date, a face, a place. Not a generic backstory.
- The Target must be human and identifiable, not an abstract organization. Even when the Target is a structure (a department, a frat, a corporate node), it must have a face the Avenger meets.
- The Reckoning happens on-screen. The audience must witness the meeting.
- The ending is bleak or ambiguous. No triumph. No clean closure. No sequel posture.
- Restraint in scoring. Music is sparse, often diegetic. Silence carries the show.
- The Avenger's competence is plausible — military, medical, technical, social — not exceptional. Patience is the superpower, not skill.


## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Tonal register — wordless-vérité (Blue Ruin), elliptical-melancholic (The Limey), chilly-prestige (Promising Young Woman), near-comic-procedural (The Killer), gothic-stylized (Hannibal).
- The Avenger's competence base — ex-military, ex-medical, ex-cop, ex-criminal, civilian-with-trade (mechanic, accountant), socially-skilled-only.
- The Wound's exact nature — child killed, sibling assaulted, partner murdered, institutional betrayal, friend pushed to suicide, professional ruined.
- The Target's domain — single individual (most), small criminal cell, a frat, a precinct, a family.
- The Plan's mechanism — hunt, infiltration, social entrapment, financial dismantling, public exposure, direct violence.
- The Tether's relation — child, sibling, former lover, new friend, the victim's surviving relative.
- POV technique — taciturn third-person (Blue Ruin), inner monologue (The Killer, Hannibal), ensemble with the Avenger embedded (Hold the Dark).
- Period and place — modern, post-war, isolated rural, urban, international.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the plan spends the Tether; the Echo Victim exposes the Target's routine; the paid costs constrain the Reckoning) — every wound, tool, routine, and final act varies freely.

## Caveats
- Common failure mode: action-movie creep. The Avenger turns into John Wick; the Plan becomes a setpiece tour. This breaks the recipe. Tonal floor is restraint; if the violence escalates, the genre changes.
- Common failure mode: redemption through the Reckoning. The act heals the Avenger; they walk away whole. This is action-cinema's contract, not quiet revenge's. The Wound stays.
- Common failure mode: making the Target a cartoon (smirking abuser, monologuing villain). The genre's argument is that monsters are ordinary. The Target should be banal — small, defended, deniable.
- Common failure mode: the Tether becomes a romance subplot that survives the runtime. The Tether's function is to embody cost. If they're present and unscathed at the end, the cost wasn't paid.
- Register dial: how much the Wound is named in dialogue vs implied. Blue Ruin says almost nothing; Promising Young Woman names it precisely. Both work. The recipe holds; the volume varies.
- Pacing trap: the Long Wait is essential but can become inert. The discipline is to make stillness legible — let the audience read the Avenger's interior from the way they hold a cup, watch a door, breathe. Stillness is content, not absence.
- Antagonist discipline note: the Target's motive must be human and small. Not "represents toxic masculinity" — was entitled, wanted what he wanted, was protected by friends, lied. Locatable, biographical, ordinary.
- Genre-of-one risk: none. The slot is small but durable, with feature and limited-series exemplars across decades.
