# Political Satire (with Bite)

## Slot
Comedy and drama where AN INSTITUTION OF POWER renders itself RIDICULOUS through the daily mechanics of operating it, and the protagonists — politicians, staffers, advisors, journalists, contractors — are COMPLICIT in the absurdity, not above it. The laughter is from recognition; the bite is that nothing improves; the protagonist's own compromise is part of the joke.

## Corpus
- *Veep* (HBO) — White House comedy, acid-Iannucci
- *In the Loop* (Iannucci) — Anglo-American war prelude, foundational-acid
- *The Thick of It* (BBC) — UK ministerial farce, foundational
- *The Death of Stalin* (Iannucci) — Soviet politburo, period-acid
- *Yes Minister* (BBC) — Whitehall, classical-dry
- *Don't Look Up* (McKay) — climate / media, broad-satirical
- *House of Cards (UK original)* (BBC, 1990) — succession plot, gothic-satirical
- *Succession* (HBO) — media empire, satirical-prestige
- *Mr. Mayor* (NBC) — local politics, network-comedy
- *Parks and Recreation* (lean) — municipal politics, warm-satirical
- *Avenue 5* (HBO) — Iannucci in space, lighter-acid
- *Black Mirror* "The National Anthem" — UK PM crisis, satirical-horror
- *JFK* (Stone) — political conspiracy, paranoid-satirical
- *The Loudest Voice* (Showtime) — Fox News / Roger Ailes, drama-with-satirical-frame
- *Civil War* (Garland) — war reporting in collapse, satirical-elegiac

(Tonal range: from acid-Iannucci through classical-Yes Minister, broad-McKay, warm-Parks, paranoid-Stone, to elegiac-Garland. Same recipe — power's self-ridiculing operation — different registers of how much the satire bleeds.)

## Emotional contract
**Recognition + complicity + futility.** The audience laughs at the absurdity of power and recognizes their own implication — in voting, watching, working, scrolling. The bite is that none of it gets better; the relief is being told the truth.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Petty Crisis at Scale** — A minor humiliation (gaffe, bad photo, viral clip, mispronounced name, leaked email) consumes the institution; meanwhile the actual crisis (war, climate, vote, layoffs) continues unaddressed. Iannucci's bread and butter.
- **The Walk-and-Curse** — Walk-and-talk borrowed from West Wing but inverted: profane, sweating, panicking. Senior Staffer dragging Junior through corridors while incinerating subordinates. Genre signature.
- **The Speech That Lies** — The Principal delivers a public statement; the audience knows the truth from the previous scene; the gap is the laugh. Cut between podium and back room.
- **The Meeting That Fails** — Multi-character negotiation where no one is listening, everyone has an agenda, the loudest voice wins, and the policy outcome is determined by ego and lunchtime.
- **The Complicit Joke** — A protagonist makes a self-aware joke about the awful thing they just did; the joke acknowledges the awfulness and proceeds. The genre's posture toward its own characters.

**Supporting tropes (stack 3-6 per work):**
- **The Briefing That Goes Wrong** — The Principal is briefed; doesn't read; misremembers; says the wrong thing in public; Spinner mops up.
- **The Leak** — Anonymous information escapes; Senior Staffer must find the source; usually one of them did it strategically.
- **The Operational Cock-Up** — A small logistical failure (wrong restaurant booked, wrong delegation flown, wrong document published) cascades into a major event.
- **The Sycophant Subordinate** — A character whose job is agreement; vehicle for the Principal's worst instincts.
- **The Outside Expert Ignored** — A scientist, general, economist, or activist tells the truth in a room; is dismissed because politically inconvenient.
- **The Press Conference** — Set-piece; the institution's image-management on display; questions dodged, lies delivered, gaffe risks live.
- **The Hotel Room / Hospitality Suite** — Decisions made informally over drinks, away from the official venue; the actual locus of power.
- **The Awkward Photo** — A photograph of the Principal in compromising posture (eating wrong, with wrong person, doing absurd thing) defines a news cycle.
- **The Election Night / Confidence Vote / Board Meeting** — Periodic accountability event; rigged or survived; rarely changes substance.
- **The Foreign Counterpart** — A leader from another country / institution mirrors the absurdity in their own register; bilateral farce.
- **The Whistleblower Subplot** — Someone inside is leaking truth; Senior Staffer or Spinner hunts them; usually neutralized.
- **The Statement of Sincere Belief** — Rare beat where a character genuinely believes in something; the institution makes them choose between belief and position; they choose position.
- **The Body Count Reveal** — Late in the season / film, the cost of the absurdity is shown — civilian dead, layoffs final, planet visibly worsened. The bite.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't have the protagonists fix the institution. Reform is not the recipe's payoff; the satire's bite depends on persistence.
- Don't make the Principal a competent secret genius. The vanity-and-cowardice mix is the engine. House of Cards's Urquhart / Underwood is the satirical-gothic edge case; their competence is at acquiring power, not at governing.
- Don't redeem the cynical Spinner. They can crack; they don't reform. Veep's posture: everyone is who you thought they were.
- Don't moralize from outside the satire. The genre's strength is that the writers are inside the joke; sanctimonious framing breaks the form.
- Don't let the protagonists outsmart the system from the inside permanently. Their wins are tactical, the system is structural.
- Don't show the public as wise. The genre is about the wretched performance of representation, not the wisdom of the people being represented. Veep's voters are equally absurd.
- Don't soften the Body. The cost-bearer must register; if it doesn't, the satire is toothless. Iannucci puts a dead soldier at the end of In the Loop for a reason.

## Conformity rules
- The institution must persist at the end. Reform is not the genre's payoff; satire requires that the absurdity continue. Even House of Cards's protagonist falls into a more cynical successor.
- The protagonists must be COMPLICIT. The Junior Staffer must compromise on screen; the audience must watch them do it. Sanctimonious protagonists break the form.
- The Petty Crisis at Scale must operate alongside the real one. The genre's central joke is that institutions cannot tell which is which; the show must enact that.
- The Body — the cost-bearer of the absurdity — must register at least once per season. Even Veep eventually counts dead soldiers, lost jobs, broken constituents. Without the cost, the satire becomes only fun.
- Specific institutional texture required — real procedural detail (whip operations, briefing flow, press operations, board mechanics, succession rules). Vague satire is weak satire.
- The genre's posture is from inside the room, not from outside. The camera goes WITH the staff, not AT them. Outside-only POV (pure outraged citizen) breaks the form.
- At least one Speech That Lies and one Meeting That Fails per episode/act. The set-pieces are the recipe's beats.

- Common failure mode: the Principal becomes an admirable trickster, the show tilts toward power-fantasy; House of Cards (US) drifted here. Correction: the Principal's costs to others must remain visible.
- Common failure mode: the Junior Staffer is too principled. They never compromise; the show becomes a bildungsroman of integrity. The genre needs the compromise on screen. Audience must watch the principles bend.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Institution — White House (Veep, Loudest Voice), Whitehall (Thick of It, Yes Minister), Politburo (Death of Stalin), media empire (Succession), parliament (House of Cards UK), city hall (Mr. Mayor, Parks and Rec), space cruise (Avenue 5), climate / media (Don't Look Up), broadcast network (Loudest Voice), war zone (Civil War).
- Tonal register — acid-Iannucci, classical-dry (Yes Minister), broad-McKay (Don't Look Up), warm-comic (Parks, Mr. Mayor), gothic-satirical (House of Cards UK), prestige-drama (Succession, Loudest Voice), elegiac (Civil War).
- Era — contemporary (Veep, Avenue 5, Don't Look Up), period (Death of Stalin, Loudest Voice), near-future (Black Mirror), collapsed-future (Civil War).
- Profession of the Junior Staffer POV — political staffer, journalist, lawyer, intern, military aide, scientific advisor, communications officer, intelligence briefer.
- Whether the satire targets the left, the right, or both — Iannucci's discipline is to skewer everyone in the room; partisan satire is a different register and tends to lose bite. Don't Look Up takes a side; Veep refuses to.
- Whether the Principal is the protagonist (House of Cards) or the staff are (Veep, Thick of It, Yes Minister). Both work; the first leans gothic, the second leans farce.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (image management suppresses process, compromise protects the lie, and procedural failure lands on the Body) — every institution, error, statement, and absorbed exposure varies freely.

## Caveats
- The genre's primary risk is becoming partisan cheering rather than satire. Both sides must register as absurd in their own ways; one-sided satire is propaganda with jokes.
- The genre's second primary risk is becoming nihilistic — laughing at everything with no anchor. The Body, the cost-bearer, is the anchor. Without them, the comedy floats free and the audience stops caring.
- Specific real-world political moments inevitably date the work. Veep aged poorly in places not because the satire failed but because reality outran it. Treat date-stamping as feature, not bug, but plan for it.
- Drama-leaning works (Loudest Voice, Civil War, JFK) are satire-shaped without being comedies. The recipe's bones — institution rendering itself ridiculous through its own operation — still apply; the register dial is moved toward gravity.
