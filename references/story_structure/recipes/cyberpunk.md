# Cyberpunk (Alienation + Rage + Defiance)

## Slot
Near-future serialized fiction where a marginal protagonist — gig laborer, mercenary, ex-cop, contract programmer, sex worker, runaway — operates inside a corporate-saturated city that has commodified every part of them (body, attention, memory, time) and gets pulled into a confrontation with the System they are already complicit in. The genre is technological intimacy at the bottom of the food chain. The streets feel lived-in; the towers feel inhuman; the protagonist is wired into both.

- *Blade Runner 2049* (Villeneuve)
- *The Matrix* (Wachowskis)
- *Ghost in the Shell* (Oshii, 1995 / SAC)
- *Akira* (Otomo)
- *Cyberpunk: Edgerunners* (Trigger / CDPR)
- *Altered Carbon* (Netflix / Morgan)
- *Deus Ex* (Eidos / Spector)
- *Cyberpunk 2077* (CDPR)
- *RoboCop* (Verhoeven)
- *Alita: Battle Angel* (Rodriguez / Kishiro)
- *Mr. Robot* (USA / Esmail)
- *Black Mirror* — "San Junipero," "USS Callister," "Smithereens"
- *Snow Crash* (Stephenson)
- *Strange Days* (Bigelow)

(Tonal range: from Edgerunners' pulp-tragic kinetic and Cyberpunk 2077's gonzo-pulp through Akira and Ghost in the Shell's animated body-horror to Blade Runner 2049's prestige-philosophical hush and Mr. Robot's paranoid-prestige. Same recipe — protagonist wired into the system that owns them.)

## Emotional contract
**Alienation + rage + defiance.** Audience enters a world where every surface is sold, every relationship is mediated by tech they don't own, and the protagonist is already a product. The rage builds slowly as the protagonist sees their own complicity. Defiance is the cocktail's payoff — not victory, not escape, but the act of refusing to be the thing the System designed them to be. Often paid for in the body.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Body Is Inventory** — Limbs, eyes, neural ports, memories, sleeve-skins are commodities. The protagonist's status is legible in their hardware. Upgrades cost; downgrades hurt. The body is the show's economy made visible.
- **The Job That Goes Wrong** — Pilot opens with a routine gig (extraction, courier run, hack, hit, contract). It blows in act one and exposes a layer the protagonist wasn't supposed to see. The job becomes the season.
- **The Sold Self** — Some core piece of the protagonist has already been sold or rented before the story starts (memory, organ, time, allegiance). The audience learns the price as the story unfolds.
- **The Quiet Defiance** — The protagonist's climactic act is not toppling the Corp — it's a refusal that the System can't process. A leak, a destroyed file, a saved child, a kept name, a final fuck-you that scars the record.
- **Rain on the City** — The world's visual texture is wet, neon, vertical, polyglot, layered. Streets crowded, towers empty. Weather is mood and class.

**Supporting tropes:**
- **The Ripperdoc Scene** — A back-alley body shop where the protagonist gets installed / patched / upgraded. The cost is always more than the price.
- **Chrome Sickness** — Late-stage augmentation produces dissociation, hallucination, identity erosion. The augmentations work and they are killing you (Edgerunners cyberpsychosis, Akira's tumor logic, Deus Ex's biochip rejection).
- **The Glitch Truth** — A piece of evidence the Corp tries to scrub keeps surfacing — a corrupted file, a memory that won't compile, a ghost in the network. The genre's whistleblower.
- **The Synthetic Question** — Someone in the cast may not be biologically human — replicant, AI, uploaded mind, clone, sleeve. The genre refuses to settle the question cleanly; the answer changes nothing about whether they matter.
- **The Megacorp Logo** — A single corporation looms over the city, on every product, on the protagonist's gear, in the rain itself (Tyrell, Arasaka, E-Corp, Wallace, Weyland). Brand as gravity.
- **The Slum and the Tower** — Vertical class. The protagonist lives at the bottom, the antagonist works at the top, and the elevator is the season's geography.
- **The Black ICE Run** — A hack, infiltration, or run into a forbidden network where the cost of failure is brain-death. The genre's chase sequence.
- **The Lower-Class Family** — Sibling, parent, kid, partner who is NOT in the System's war and is being ground anyway. The Anchor lives here.
- **The Replicant Test** — A diagnostic — Voight-Kampff, Turing prompt, memory probe — that determines someone's category and license to live. The genre's interrogation scene.
- **The Sleeve / Soul Split** — Mind and body have come apart commercially (Altered Carbon stacks, Ghost in the Shell ghosts, Matrix avatars, Edgerunners' chrome). Selfhood is a contract.
- **The Burned Manifesto** — A speech, broadcast, document, or song the protagonist transmits at the climax that says the thing the System has been laundering. Often the protagonist's last act.
- **The Rooftop Conversation** — Wired Protagonist and Hollow Operator or Corporate Face meet high above the city for a long talk. Class made literal. Often the genre's moral confrontation.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't have the Corp be defeated. The recipe is defiance, not revolution. The Corp persists; the protagonist scars the record.
- Don't make the protagonist a pure outsider — they must be implicated. If they're a clean-handed rebel from outside the System, the genre slides into YA dystopian.
- Don't resolve the Synthetic Question. If the show declares "yes she was a real human / no he was just a program" cleanly, the genre's dignity argument collapses.
- Don't make the Corporate Face stupid or cartoonishly evil — they must read as competent, articulate, and dangerously plausible. They must have a worldview.
- Don't let the augmentations be cost-free. Every upgrade owes. If the protagonist gets stronger without paying, the genre evaporates into superhero.
- Don't end clean. The protagonist is dead, broken, hollowed, exiled, contaminated, or compromised. Defiance without cost reads as fantasy.
- Don't explain the world through monologue. Rain, signage, ad bombardment, polyglot crowds, broken infrastructure — the City teaches; the script does not exposit.

## Conformity rules
- The protagonist must be IMPLICATED in the System at the start — paid by it, using its tech, owed by it, owing it. A clean-hands hero is the wrong genre.
- The City must be a CHARACTER — rain, neon, vertical class, polyglot signage, ad bombardment, broken infrastructure, weather that smells like solvent. Worldbuilding through environment, not dialogue.
- The body must pay. Augmentation, hacking, running, fighting — every capability owes. Chrome Sickness or its equivalent must manifest at least once.
- The Megacorp must persist at the end. Defiance, not revolution. The protagonist scars the record; the tower stays lit.
- The Synthetic Question (who counts as human / sentient / worthy) must be raised and must NOT be resolved cleanly. Ambiguity is the genre's dignity.
- The Anchor must exist on screen. Defiance without a specific named loss reads as posturing.
- The protagonist's final act must cost — death, breakage, exile, hollowing, contamination. If they walk away whole and free, the recipe has failed.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Setting register — neon-Hong-Kong pastiche (Blade Runner), animated body-horror Tokyo (Akira / Ghost), Eastern Bloc grit (Cyberpunk 2077 / Edgerunners' Night City), surveillance-Manhattan (Mr. Robot), interstellar-class (Altered Carbon), American suburbia rendered cyber (Black Mirror).
- Augmentation register — wet biotech (Strange Days, Ghost), chrome (Edgerunners, Alita), digital sleeves (Altered Carbon), neural-only (Matrix, Mr. Robot), AI-companion (BR2049's Joi, Her-adjacent).
- The protagonist's job — courier, merc, hacker, ex-cop, sex worker, idol, gig laborer, journalist, soldier. Each register dial.
- Tonal register — pulp-kinetic (Edgerunners, Akira, Cyberpunk 2077), prestige-philosophical (Blade Runner 2049, Ghost in the Shell), paranoid-procedural (Mr. Robot, Strange Days), animated-tragic (Edgerunners), grindhouse (RoboCop, original Blade Runner).
- Whether the Corp wins big, wins small, or notices the scar — register dial. Edgerunners has the Corp barely notice; Mr. Robot has the System genuinely shaken; BR2049 has the Corp untouched. All conform to the recipe.
- Whether the Anchor is human, AI, or hybrid — the bond is the floor; the substrate is the variable.
- Whether the protagonist survives — Akira's Kaneda lives, Edgerunners' David dies, Mr. Robot's Elliot fragments. All recipe-valid.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the Anchor is hit through a sold name and a refused shelter; the run collects the chrome's bill; the Manifesto consumes the tap, the truth, and the refusal) — the CONTENT of every edge varies freely: who sells the name, what was sold before the story, which chrome, what the broadcast says.

## Caveats
- Common failure mode: the protagonist as righteous outsider. The recipe collapses if the protagonist isn't complicit at the start. Edgerunners' David is a corpo kid; Mr. Robot's Elliot works for E-Corp; Deckard hunts replicants; Alita is a Corp's weapon before she's a person. Complicity is the engine. (Now encoded in the wiring: E1's pocketed glitch and E4's chrome-on-credit are the complicity firing as choices.)
- Common failure mode: explaining the City. The genre's worldbuilding is environmental — signage, weather, ad layers, polyglot crowds, infrastructure rot. Voiceover monologue describing "the year is 2099" kills the recipe instantly.
- Common failure mode: clean defiance. If the protagonist's final act overthrows the Corp and the people are free, the genre slides into YA dystopian. Defiance is small, costly, and SCARS the record without ending the System. (Now encoded in the wiring: E10 consumes the death warrant and the body's tremor alongside the broadcast.)
- Register dial: complicity intensity. Edgerunners has the protagonist deeply employed by the System; Mr. Robot has them functionally inside it while pretending not to be; Blade Runner has them quietly serving. The recipe demands complicity above some threshold — set the dial deliberately.
- Tonal dial: the Synthetic Question can be the spine (Blade Runner, Ghost in the Shell) or a subplot (Edgerunners, Mr. Robot). Either works; it must be present.
- Genre proximity: superhero stories give cost-free augmentation; cyberpunk does not. If the body never pays, the recipe has slid. (Now encoded in the wiring: E4's install owes, E8 collects mid-run, E10 settles in the body.)
