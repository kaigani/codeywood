# Western (Road + Lone Rider + Showdown)

## Slot
Serialized western where a single rider — drifter, lawman, hunter, returning veteran, exile, fugitive — moves across a vast and morally exhausted landscape, encountering episodic figures and small communities, accumulating obligation and threat, building toward an inevitable confrontation. The land is post-rule or pre-rule; the protagonist is partly its agent and partly its symptom. Pays in the slow burn of accumulated weight, the texture of small towns and long rides, and the violence that arrives when arrived at.

## Corpus
- *The Searchers* (Ford)
- *Hell or High Water* (Mackenzie)
- *No Country for Old Men* (Coen Brothers)
- *The Power of the Dog* (Campion)
- *There Will Be Blood* (Anderson)
- *Hostiles* (Cooper)
- *Meek's Cutoff* (Reichardt)
- *Dead Man* (Jarmusch)
- *The Sisters Brothers* (Audiard)
- *First Cow* (Reichardt)
- *Damnation* (USA Network)
- *Godless* (Netflix)
- *Deadwood* (HBO, lean episodes)
- *True Grit* (Coens)
- *Logan* (Mangold)

(Tonal range: The Searchers' classical-myth and True Grit's adventure-classical through Meek's Cutoff's austere-revisionist and Dead Man's mythic-strange to There Will Be Blood's prestige-monstrous and No Country's revisionist-bleak. Same recipe — lone rider, road, episodic encounters, showdown earned across a season.)

## Emotional contract
**Drift + accumulation + reckoning.** The audience moves with a rider across a landscape that does not hurry and watches them carry weight (vengeance, obligation, debt, grief, mission) toward a confrontation everyone — protagonist, antagonist, audience — can see coming. The cocktail is the long burn: small encounters, gathered intelligence, deferred violence, then payoff. Catharsis is the showdown; the texture is the road.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Arrival in Town** — Rider enters a settlement — saloon, station, homestead. Town reads them; they read the town. Brief scene establishes who is dangerous, who is friendly, what's owed. Repeated 3-5 times across the season; each town is its own short story.
- **The Road / The Ride** — Travel sequences between towns — vista, weather, fatigue, small dialogue with the Companion, fire at night. The genre's procedural rhythm. Time is shown to pass.
- **The Inevitable Showdown** — Climactic confrontation between Rider and Quarry — face to face, often outdoors, often with witnesses or alone. Staged with full aesthetic commitment — light, distance, weapons drawn, time slowed. Earned across the season.
- **The Old Debt Revealed** — Backstory — the original act — surfaced through conversation, found object, an old photograph, a remembered name. The audience earns the why through assembly.
- **The Witness / Survivor of the Quarry** — A person the Quarry has harmed — widow, orphan, scarred survivor, traumatized child — encountered by the Rider, used to confirm the Quarry's evil and to raise the stake.

**Supporting tropes:**
- **The Saloon Scene** — Establishment shot — bar, piano, card game, suspicion. The Rider's first read of the town; the town's first read of them.
- **The Campfire Confession** — Rider, Companion, or a fellow traveler shares part of their past at a fire. Slow exposition vehicle; bonding scene.
- **The Tracking Sequence** — Rider follows physical evidence — hoofprints, smoke, broken twig, a body. Genre's procedural craft.
- **The Lawman's Warning** — Local Sheriff or marshal tells the Rider to step off — jurisdictional, weary, often genuine. Frames the Rider's defiance as choice.
- **The Stagecoach / Train / Riverboat** — Encounter with a moving vehicle that carries an episodic stranger (a doctor, a rival, a corpse, a witness). Brief sequence; usually one episode's frame.
- **The Native Encounter** — Indigenous figure(s) — guide, war party, family, exile — handled with varying degrees of revisionism. (Recipe note: revisionist westerns treat as full characters; classical westerns require careful inheritance work.)
- **The Brothel / Boarding House Scene** — Madam, hostess, or boarding house keeper — older woman with information; sometimes lover, sometimes confidant, sometimes employer of the Companion.
- **The Wound Treated** — Rider injured in early encounter; treated by Companion, doctor, sympathetic woman. Recurrence of the body's vulnerability across the road.
- **The Money / The Object** — A bag, a horse, a letter, a body, a child, a deed — physical thing the Rider is carrying or pursuing. Concrete grammar of the obligation.
- **The Massacre / Aftermath Scene** — Burned cabin, butchered cattle, dead family, hanged man. Confirms the Quarry's violence; raises the stake.
- **The Funeral on the Plain** — Burial in the open — Companion, ally, stranger — handled with quiet ritual. The genre's signature image.
- **The False Authority** — Lawman, judge, sheriff, or landowner who is in fact the Quarry's accomplice or proxy. Discovered mid-season.
- **The River Crossing** — Specific geographic obstacle — river, canyon, pass, badlands — that has to be negotiated. Mortality and labor made visible.
- **The Quarry Glimpsed** — Distant sighting of the antagonist — across a valley, in a saloon mirror, on a far ridge, in a photograph. Tension architecture.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't make the Rider invulnerable. The wound, the fatigue, the doubt — required. Action-hero invincibility is the wrong genre.
- Don't reveal the Old Debt in a single dump. The audience earns it through Campfire Confession, found object, Witness encounter, a name spoken in a Saloon. Monologue exposition kills the burn.
- Don't have the Quarry be a generic outlaw. Their conviction must be coherent. The audience must understand why they think they're right (even if monstrously).
- Don't end with the town saved and the system restored. The recipe ends with the showdown's cost; the system that allowed the Quarry persists. Restoration is the wrong recipe (classical sub-variant excepted; even there the cost lingers).
- Don't keep the Landscape generic. Specific named geography — the Pecos, the Powder River, the Llano Estacado, a fictional named valley — required. "The West" abstracted is the wrong genre.
- Don't kill the Companion casually. Their loss must be the season's emotional knife or they survive. Disposable companions belong to action.
- Don't resolve the showdown by ambush from offscreen. The confrontation must be face-to-face, staged, with both figures seeing each other. The genre's contract.

## Conformity rules
- The Rider must be alone or in a dyad with a Companion. Posse-westerns belong to a different recipe; the lone-road register requires solitary motion.
- The Landscape must be specific — named geography, recurring environmental details, weather and terrain as character. Generic "West" undermines the recipe.
- The Showdown must be face-to-face, staged, with both figures aware of each other. Offscreen kills or ambushes belong to thriller.
- The Old Debt must exist and must be revealed gradually. A Rider with no past is a tourist; the past IS the gravity.
- The Quarry must be coherent — articulate, defensible from their own frame, dangerous beyond simple criminality. Cardboard outlaws kill the recipe.
- The Town(s) must be specific — a saloon, a sheriff, a madam, a banker, named figures. Generic frontier-template kills atmosphere.
- The cost must be paid — Companion lost, Rider permanently changed, system unchanged. Restoration to normal is the wrong genre.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Era and setting — classical (1870s-1890s frontier), modern western (No Country, Hell or High Water — contemporary Texas / Southwest), period-hybrid (Logan — near-future neo-western), prestige-historical (The Power of the Dog — 1920s Montana, Hostiles — 1890s closing-frontier). Recipe constant; the saddle becomes a truck.
- Quarry type — outlaw, oil baron, corrupt lawman, rival rancher, brother, religious patriarch, drug runner (modern). Each shifts moral grammar.
- Rider's profession — drifter, lawman, hunter, returning veteran, oil prospector, bank robber, ranger, undertaker, doctor. Each shifts the tool of trade.
- The Companion — child, woman, sibling, native guide, junior, witness, doctor, prostitute. Each shifts the protective stake.
- Tonal register — classical-myth, revisionist-bleak, prestige-monstrous, mythic-strange (Dead Man), austere-revisionist (Meek's), adventure-classical (True Grit). Recipe scales.
- Outcome — Rider survives changed (most common), Rider dies arrived (Dead Man, There Will Be Blood-variant), Rider walks off ambiguous (Searchers' last shot). All valid.
- Geographic flavor — desert, plain, mountain, oil-field, dying coastal town, Montana valley, Texas border. Each is a tonal dial.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the road emits a witness, a bargain, a wound, a route, and a debt the back half must consume) — the CONTENT of every edge varies freely: what the object is, who needs protection, how the town is compromised, and what exact country the showdown is fought in.

## Caveats
- Register dial: classical-myth runs warm and clean; revisionist-bleak runs cold and ambiguous; prestige-monstrous extends the burn across long takes. All compatible with the 10-episode spine.
- Genre proximity: the "modern western" — No Country, Hell or High Water, Logan — keeps the recipe almost exactly intact while updating the saddle. Truck = horse; deputy = sheriff; cartel = outlaw band. The recipe survives modernization remarkably well.
- TV expansion challenge: 10 episodes of road requires meaningful episodic encounters (Towns, Stagecoaches, Native Encounters, Witness scenes). Damnation, Godless, Deadwood show how to expand; the recipe's chapters are the Towns visited.
- Failure mode: posse vs lone-road blur. If the Rider has a permanent crew of 4+, the recipe slides into ensemble western (different recipe — Magnificent Seven, Bone Tomahawk in places). Lone-road requires the Rider to be the gravity. The Companion is allowed; the crew is not.
- Failure mode: classical-vs-revisionist confusion. Both work; choose deliberately. Classical: the Rider restores moral order at cost (Searchers). Revisionist: the Rider IS the cost and the order doesn't get restored (No Country, There Will Be Blood). Mid-tone is harder; the recipe scales to both ends.
- Failure mode: Native peoples handled badly. The recipe requires care — revisionist westerns treat indigenous characters as full agents; classical westerns can be inherited critically. Default to revisionist craft.
- Failure mode: showdown via ambush. The recipe requires staged confrontation. Offscreen / sniped kills belong to crime thriller; the western's contract is the standoff. (Now encoded in the wiring: the final beat consumes the showdown ground, the counted rounds, and the named debt in a direct face-to-face.)
