# Teen Intimacy Confession

## Slot
Teen-focused romance / coming-of-age drama where the structural pivot is a FIRST major emotional or sexual disclosure between teen protagonists — the moment of saying the thing, showing the body, naming the desire, admitting the truth — in a world that is unsafe and yet whose unsafeness does not invalidate the disclosure. Commercial neighborhood: the Heartstopper / Sex Education / Lady Bird / Eighth Grade / Never Have I Ever shelf — teen-tier intimacy where the confession (and the becoming it precipitates) is the engine, not the romance plot per se.

## Emotional contract
**Vulnerability + becoming + risk.** The audience pays to feel the size of a small confession — the moment a teen tells another teen the truth (about desire, sexuality, identity, a wound, a name) and discovers the world does not end and they do not end and they are now someone slightly different. The recipe makes the SMALL DISCLOSURE feel as large as it actually is to the teen experiencing it. Pleasure is in being met.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone — the recipe turns on at least two):**
- **The Confession Beat** — A scene where the protagonist says the thing — names the crush, the identity, the wound, the desire — to the Receiver. The recipe's structural pivot. The dialogue is often plain, the staging often small (a bedroom, a parked car, a school hallway after hours, a text thread). The genre's nucleus.
- **The Almost-Disclosure** — Multiple prior scenes where the protagonist nearly says it and doesn't. Each near-miss raises the size of the eventual disclosure. The recipe lives in these.

**Supporting tropes (stack 4-6 per work):**
- **The Rehearsal With the Best Friend** — The protagonist tries the sentence out on the Best Friend before the Receiver. The audience hears it twice; the second time it lands differently.
- **The Text Bubble / DM / Read Receipt** — Asynchronous near-confession via screen. The phone is the genre's primary architecture. Typing, deleting, the three dots, the seen-no-reply.
- **The Bedroom Scene** — A teen's bedroom as the recipe's safe space — posters, light, the closed door, the parent who knocks. Many disclosures happen here.
- **The Party** — A house party / school dance / sleepover as compression chamber. Alcohol or its absence; the closeness of bodies; the bathroom mirror. One pivot scene per season.
- **The First-Time Sequence** — First kiss, first sex, first hand-hold, first I-love-you. Specific and small. The genre treats the first-time as architecture, not titillation.
- **The Body Self-Check** — A scene of the protagonist alone with their body — mirror, shower, locker room, dressing. The body's reality is part of the disclosure.
- **The Parent Half-Knows** — A scene where a parent ALMOST gets it — a look, a question, a sentence not finished. The Hostile Surface flickers. Sometimes the parent steps forward; sometimes they retreat.
- **The Adult Who Said the Right Thing Once** — A teacher, coach, nurse, neighbor delivers one line, then exits. The line is remembered for the rest of the season.
- **The Friend Group at the Lunch Table** — The micro-community: the seating, the in-jokes, the absence when someone is missing. The recipe's social geography.
- **The Mortifying Public Moment** — A teen-specific humiliation (the wrong text sent, the rumor, the parent dropping them off in front of friends, the public crying jag, the period). Often the third act's pivot.
- **The Confession That Goes Wrong** — A first disclosure that is NOT met — laughed at, weaponized, ignored. The recipe sometimes uses this BEFORE the eventual met-disclosure; the contrast is the engine.
- **The Soft Resurrection of the Friendship** — A scene late in the season where the Best Friend is restored after rupture. The recipe protects the friendship as the long arc.
- **The Symbol Object** — A specific small object that holds the secret (a Polaroid, a playlist, a borrowed jacket, a notebook, a friendship bracelet). The genre's anchors.
- **The Diary / Journal / Voice Memo** — The protagonist's interiority externalized. Sometimes voiceover; sometimes shown as written text on screen.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't moralize. The recipe does not deliver a lesson; the disclosure is the event, not its proof of value.
- Don't make the Hostile Surface a single named villain. The pressure is diffuse, ambient, structural. Singling it into one bully or one parent flattens the recipe.
- Don't have the adult solve the problem. The Soft Adult witnesses; the teens do the work. Adult rescue invalidates the becoming.
- Don't sexualize the body for the audience's gaze. Teen-tier first-time sequences are specific to the teen's experience; the camera is theirs, not ours.
- Don't punish disclosure. The recipe's ethic is that saying the thing is survivable; even when it goes wrong, it is recoverable.
- Don't resolve the Specific Wound with explanation. The wound is integrated, not cured. The parent who left does not return and apologize. The body the protagonist hated is not magically loved. The change is in the protagonist's relationship to it.
- Don't write the Best Friend as a sidekick. They have their own arc, even if it's quiet.

## Conformity rules
- The disclosure must be SMALL and SPECIFIC. The recipe does not work on abstract "trauma" or generic "identity"; the protagonist must have a sentence they have not yet said.
- The audience must know what the protagonist is carrying before the protagonist says it. Dramatic irony is the recipe's tempo.
- The Hostile Surface must be DIFFUSE, not singular. Singling it into one bully or one parent flattens the recipe into bully-of-the-week.
- The Soft Adult must exist but must NOT solve the problem. Witnessing is the adult's job.
- The Receiver must meet the disclosure. The recipe permits a wrong-first-disclosure (episode 6) but the structural confession (episode 8) must be met. Otherwise the recipe is a different genre (teen tragedy).
- The Best Friend's arc must be protected. The romance is layered on top of the friendship, not under it.
- The teen perspective is sovereign. The camera is theirs. Adult readings of teen experience belong elsewhere.
- The genre's most common failure is making the disclosure feel SMALL to the audience because the writer treated it as small. The recipe's pleasure is the asymmetry — small to the world, enormous to the teen. If the writer doesn't honor the size, the audience doesn't either.
- The second-most-common failure is making the Hostile Surface a single bully or a single bad parent. This collapses the recipe into older bully-arc shapes; the modern teen-intimacy recipe needs the pressure to be ambient and structural.
- The third common failure is adult rescue — having a parent or teacher solve what the teen is trying to say. This invalidates the becoming. The Soft Adult must witness, not fix.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- The content of the disclosure — queerness, neurodivergence, a wound, an identity, a desire, a fact about the body, a religious leaving, a death. Open.
- The Receiver's role — love interest, best friend, sibling, stranger, parent. Open.
- Tonal register — sweet (Heartstopper, Half of It), cringe-comedic (Pen15, Eighth Grade), wry naturalism (Lady Bird, Edge of Seventeen, Booksmart), bruising-maximalist (Euphoria, Skins, Mid90s).
- Era and setting — contemporary digital (Heartstopper, Never Have I Ever, Atypical), pre-digital (Pen15, Mid90s, Boyhood), genre-adjacent (Yellowjackets teen tier).
- The Specific Wound's mode — present-tense (a current condition), past-tense (a prior loss), future-tense (an upcoming change).
- Whether voiceover / journal text is used as interiority — open.
- The Mortifying Public Moment's content — varies widely; should be ERA-SPECIFIC to the recipe's setting (a viral video in 2024; a passed note in 1996).
- The wiring's edges are fixed (concealment creates the cover story; reduced privacy drives the wrong disclosure; the met confession creates the boundary that costs the friendship) — every sentence, object, channel, confidence, and repair varies freely.

## Caveats
- The romance plot is OPTIONAL. The recipe runs on disclosure; the Receiver can be a best friend, a sibling, a self. Heartstopper and Eighth Grade are the same recipe, the first with a romance receiver, the second with a self-receiver.
- The recipe shades into adjacent slots — coming_of_age_indie when the disclosure is internal/cumulative rather than relational; operatic_melodrama when the disclosure is large and the world's response is structural tragedy. Hold the LINE on: disclosure is the pivot, the world is hostile-but-survivable, the camera is the teen's.
- Tonal register dial is very wide — Heartstopper and Euphoria are the same engine at opposite ends; same beats, different intensity of the Hostile Surface and the Mortifying Public Moment.
