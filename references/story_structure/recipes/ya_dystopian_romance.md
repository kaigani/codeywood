# YA Dystopian Romance (Sorted World + Forbidden Love + Reluctant Symbol)

## Slot
Serialized fiction where a teenage girl living inside an explicitly sorted society (faction, district, caste, color, tier, selection) is yanked from invisibility into the system's spotlight, falls for a structurally-wrong boy from the other side of the wall, is courted by a second boy from her old world who is becoming the revolution, and through romance-charged trials becomes — reluctantly — the symbol the system fears. The genre's argument is that in a world designed to strip individuality, **love is the most subversive act available to a body too young to vote**, and the romance is therefore the plot, not the subplot.

(Tonal range: from Hunger Games' kinetic-arena through Red Queen's court-intrigue and Divergent's faction-trial to Matched / Delirium's softer surveillance-romance and Selection's reality-show register. Same recipe — sorted girl, forbidden love, reluctant symbol — different production scale and violence ceiling.)

## Emotional contract
**Sorted world + forbidden love + reluctant symbol.** Audience opens compressed inside the sorting (factions, districts, castes), feels the heroine's invisibility, then sees her yanked into the spotlight in episode 1's act-out. The drug is two-fold and simultaneous: the romance's *charged proximity inside surveillance* (every stolen glance treason; every near-touch could be the last) and the *reluctant transformation* from broke-and-invisible to symbol-the-system-fears. The cocktail's release is the late-stage love confession under duress + the public-defiance gesture that cracks the system without bringing it down. Cliffhanger reveal pulls the audience into the next season the way the first YA dystopian novels pulled readers into book two.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone — every YA dystopian romance bestseller uses these):**
- **The Sorting Mechanism** — Factions, districts, castes, colors, selections, tributes, types — the world divides its population by a system that pretends to be natural and is actually political. The sort tells the heroine who she is supposed to be; the entire story is her rejecting that assignment. Generates all conflict; lets the audience see the injustice before the heroine does (dramatic irony).
- **The Reluctant Chosen One** — Test result, genetic mutation, accidental public defiance, banned ability — the heroine is revealed unique and is TERRIFIED, not thrilled; being chosen in a dystopia means being a target. The trope is the adolescent experience amplified: she didn't ask to be seen, she didn't ask for her body to matter, she didn't ask for the world to suddenly demand things from her.
- **Forbidden Love (Structural, Not Social)** — The romance is dangerous, not just frowned upon; faction loyalty makes it treason, tributes are enemies, Reds-and-Silvers don't mix. Every romantic scene could get someone killed; every conversation must happen in subtext. The forbidden element forces the couple to communicate through stolen glances and accidental contact — the proximity-and-tension grammar.
- **Trials / Games / Selection / Initiation** — Some kind of high-stakes container where the heroine must prove competence — Games, Initiation, Selection rounds, Arena, Court Season, Academy ranking. Gives immediate physical stakes, a deadline, and forces proximity with the Love Interest while she is proving she can handle herself.
- **The Rebellion She Joins Reluctantly** — There is always an underground, and the heroine does NOT start it; she is recruited, she resists, she eventually becomes its face. The rebellion gives her community + purpose, but also threatens to use her exactly like the regime does.
- **Love Confession Under Duress** — The first "I love you" never happens in a quiet moment — it happens in a bunker, a cell, a stolen five minutes before battle, during escape, when one of them might die. The genre forbids comfort.

**Supporting tropes (stack 4-6 per season):**
- **The Makeover / Public Presentation** — Stylists, ceremonies, capital wardrobes, court gowns, Silver disguise — the heroine is dressed up and paraded; the identity conflict becomes literal and visible. Cinna's fire dresses, Mare's silver disguise, Tris's tattooed initiation.
- **The Surveillance State** — Cameras, hidden mics, monitored channels, ranked dorm beds, public scoreboards — privacy is a luxury; every scene carries the threat of being watched, which makes the few unwatched scenes (rooftops, training rings after hours, locked stylist suites) precious.
- **The Practical Skill That Keeps Her Alive** — Hunting, pickpocketing, fixing, fighting, running, lying — established in episode 1, deployed across the season at escalating stakes. The skill earns the audience's "she can handle herself" before the rebellion needs her to.
- **The Mentor Who Dies** — Trainer, stylist, doctor, librarian, lieutenant — wise, weary, makes her better, then is killed / captured / exposed by the regime to prove the stakes and force her into independence.
- **The Midpoint Secret Detonates** — The Love Interest's hidden allegiance, hidden past, hidden mission, hidden killing, hidden agency-of-the-rebellion — revealed around the act-2 wall; recontextualizes every prior interaction; the audience re-reads earlier scenes.
- **The Almost-Touch Sequence** — A succession of late-night training, hand on the small of the back, fingers on a weapon corrected, lips an inch away when an alarm sounds — proximity-and-interruption is the romance's primary grammar before the late confession.
- **The Loyalist's Articulate Speech** — The Institutional Villain explains, calmly, why the system exists; the audience must briefly see the logic. The villain is never stupid.
- **The Rival's Radicalization Beat** — The Familial Rival commits an act of brutal rebellion (bombing, assassination, betrayal, casualty inflicted) that the heroine cannot endorse; the triangle hardens into a moral choice about means, not just men.
- **The Symbol Made Public** — Late-season, the heroine performs the public sign — three-finger salute, defaced anthem, hijacked broadcast, refused crown, fired arrow, name spoken aloud — that becomes the rebellion's logo. The reluctance burns off in one chosen gesture.
- **The Cliffhanger Betrayal Reveal** — Final beat of the season: the rebellion has been using her / the Love Interest knew / the Tether is captured / the Rival has done something unforgivable. Book two becomes inevitable.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't have the heroine start as a rebel. She is invisible-and-competent-and-tethered; the rebellion finds her, she resists, she is drafted. Born-revolutionaries belong to action-pulp, not YA dystopian.
- Don't make the Love Interest "nice." His structural wrongness for her is the engine; if the romance makes sense from outside, it isn't this recipe.
- Don't make the Institutional Villain cartoonish. They must articulate the regime's logic with apparent competence — the audience needs to almost see it.
- Don't put the first "I love you" in a quiet moment. The genre forbids comfort; the confession happens under duress.
- Don't let the romance unfold through dates and declarations. Proximity-and-tension is the grammar; the lovers communicate in subtext until the late confession.
- Don't resolve the triangle by killing the Rival off-screen. Both paths must feel real until the heroine chooses; the Rival is HOME, not a placeholder.
- Don't have the heroine ENJOY being chosen. The chosen-one trope is terror, not flattery — being singled out in a dystopia means being a target.
- Don't let the practical skill be a magical ability. It must be a real-world, learnable competence (hunting, pickpocketing, fighting, hacking) established before the spotlight finds her.
- Don't end the season with the regime fallen. The genre is serial; book one's climax cracks the system, the regime retaliates, and the final page makes book two inevitable.
- Don't have the Tether be a generic loved one. They must be specific, named, and vulnerable in a way the regime can weaponize.
- Don't make her awakening a single info-drop. Cracks accumulate; she sees the injustice gradually; the audience is ahead of her.

## Conformity rules
- Romance is the plot — every beat must carry the romance forward (proximity, interruption, secret, near-touch, almost-said-thing) even when the on-screen action is political. In a world designed to strip individuality, love is the most subversive act available.
- The Sorting Mechanism must be specific, visible, and lived — faction / district / caste / color / type. The audience must see how the sort touches a body before the politics is explained.
- The Heroine starts BROKE-AND-INVISIBLE with a real-world practical skill. Not chosen, not noble, not a rebel — the rebellion has not yet found her.
- The Tether must be a named, specific, vulnerable person. Generic loved-ones flatten the engine.
- The Love Interest must be structurally wrong for her, must hide a secret, and must communicate through action not declaration. His sharpness must persist through the romance.
- The Rival must come from her old world, represent home, and become the revolution while she becomes the symbol — both paths must feel real until the choice is made.
- The Institutional Villain must articulate the regime's logic with apparent competence; if the audience never sees why anyone obeys, the genre's argument collapses.
- Forbidden Love must be STRUCTURAL — faction loyalty, tribute status, blood law — not social disapproval. Every romantic scene must risk death.
- The Trials / Games / Selection container must force proximity with the Love Interest while she proves her competence. The romance unfolds inside the container.
- The first I-love-you happens under duress. The genre forbids comfortable confessions.
- The season ends with a Symbol Made Public + Cliffhanger Reveal. The regime survives, retaliating; book two is built into the final beat.
- The Mentor pays. Tactical knowledge cannot survive unscathed.
- Common failure: making the romance subordinate to the politics. If the romance can be lifted out of the season without the plot collapsing, the recipe is wrong. The romance is the plot.
- Common failure: starting the heroine post-awakening. If she sneers at the regime in episode 1, the recipe is broken; the awakening IS the show, and the first three beats are pre-awakening compression.
- Common failure: making the Love Interest emotionally available. His sharpness, his withholding, his readable-by-actions-not-words quality is structural. A communicative Love Interest collapses the proximity-and-tension grammar.
- Common failure: the love triangle as fake-out. If the Rival is obviously losing from beat one, the choice has no weight. Both paths must feel like they could be the future.
- Common failure: clean victory in the season finale. The genre is serial; cracking the system is the win; the cliffhanger is non-negotiable.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Tonal register — kinetic-violence-arena (Hunger Games, Divergent), court-intrigue-palace (Red Queen, Selection), surveillance-romance-soft (Matched, Delirium), reality-show-pageant (Selection), military-academy (An Ember in the Ashes), captive-house-marriage (Wither). Production scale varies; recipe doesn't.
- The Sort's substrate — faction (Divergent), district (Hunger Games), color / blood (Red Queen, Shatter Me), match-by-algorithm (Matched), beauty-tier (Uglies), test-result (Divergent), age-cohort (Wither). Different sort, same world-logic.
- The Container — Games / Arena (Hunger Games), Initiation (Divergent), Selection rounds (Selection, Red Queen), Academy ranking (Ember), Court Season (Red Queen), staged marriage (Wither). One of these must structure the middle.
- Violence ceiling — Hunger Games (on-screen tribute deaths, public execution) vs Matched (psychological surveillance, displacement) vs Selection (court manipulation). Adjust to platform; do not erase.
- The Tether — younger sibling (Hunger Games), parent (Divergent), best friend (Delirium), captive lover (Shatter Me), ailing dependent (Wither). One specific person.
- The Love Interest's secret — regime-soldier-with-conscience (Four), tribute-with-orders-to-protect (Peeta), prince-with-engineered-romance-show (Maxon), defected-aristocrat (Cal), hidden-power (Warner, Aaron). The specific secret varies.
- The Rival's path — childhood-friend-radicalized (Gale), tribute-from-the-same-arena (Peeta-from-Katniss-angle), former-fiance-becoming-king (Maven), district-organizer (Liam, Bracken). One of these patterns.
- Whether the heroine has supernatural-adjacent power — yes (Red Queen, Shatter Me, Darkest Minds) or no (Hunger Games, Divergent's mild Divergence, Matched, Selection). Both register. If yes, the power must terrify her.
- Ending register — system-cracked-personal-cost (Hunger Games s1, Red Queen book 1), heroine-captured-cliffhanger (Divergent's Insurgent setup), apparent-victory-rebellion-reveal (Matched's escape), Tether-killed (Mockingjay's resonance). All conform.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the personal rescue creates the draft, the secret detonates into crackdown, the symbol is chosen through romance and betrayal) — the CONTENT of every edge varies freely: which sort, whose secret, what gesture, what the Tether's danger costs.

## Caveats
- Distinct from `dystopian` recipe. The general dystopian recipe (Silo, 1984, Handmaid's, Snowpiercer, Children of Men) is romance-optional and political-awakening-centered with adult or mixed-age protagonists. YA dystopian romance is teen-specific, romance-CENTERED, and uses the love triangle + reluctant-symbol arc as the spine. Do not collapse them; the audience contract is different.
- Distinct from `romantasy`. Romantasy is fantasy-magic-immortal-court; YA dystopian romance is sci-fi or near-future / engineered-society. Magic vs system; immortal vs teen; performed cruelty vs articulate ideology.
- Distinct from `forced_together_romance`. Forced-together is a relational device that can sit inside many genres. YA dystopian romance is a content genre with a specific world-engine (sort + container + rebellion) and a specific protagonist-shape (broke + tethered + skilled + reluctant).
- Register dial: YA dystopian romance can be PG-13-arena-violence (Hunger Games) or surveillance-soft (Matched). The heart is the same; the violence ceiling is the production decision.
- This recipe leans HARD on the StoryScope `protagonist-driven resolution` axis (genre-positive in this slot — the heroine MUST make the climactic choice) and on `single causal track` (genre-positive — the romance threads every beat). The structural anti-fingerprint moves (anachrony, ambivalent moral polarity, subplot proliferation) are not the right counter-pressure inside this slot; the genre's pleasure is in the single-track inevitability of the romance under siege.
