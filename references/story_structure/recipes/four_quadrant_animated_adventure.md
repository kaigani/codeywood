# Four-Quadrant Animated Adventure

## Slot
All-ages animated feature or serialized animation where a single (or paired) protagonist pursues a clear external goal in a vivid stylized world; the emotional payoff lands for kids on the surface (visual delight, character bond) and for adults underneath (loss, identity, parental grief, growing up). Pixar's century; DreamWorks' second wind; Sony's animation department. The cocktail is wonder + risk + delight, engineered to play in the same room for everyone in the room.

## Corpus
- *How to Train Your Dragon* (DreamWorks)
- *Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse* (Sony)
- *Inside Out* (Pixar)
- *Up* (Pixar)
- *Coco* (Pixar)
- *Moana* (Disney)
- *Encanto* (Disney)
- *Wreck-It Ralph* (Disney)
- *Klaus* (Netflix / Sergio Pablos)
- *The Mitchells vs The Machines* (Sony)
- *The Bad Guys* (DreamWorks)
- *Trollhunters* (DreamWorks / Netflix)
- *The Lego Movie* (Warner / Lord-Miller)
- *Puss in Boots: The Last Wish* (DreamWorks)
- *Avatar: The Last Airbender* (Nickelodeon — lean, serialized variant)

(Tonal range: broad-kid Bad Guys / Lego through earnest-Moana / How to Train Your Dragon to prestige-Pixar Up / Coco / Inside Out. Same engine, dialed from joke-density-high to grief-density-high.)

## Emotional contract
**Wonder + risk + delight.** The audience — kid and parent in the same row — falls in love with a vivid world and a charismatic protagonist, watches the goal cost more than expected, and leaves with a single emotional theorem proven (your family is real even if it's not blood / loss makes the love permanent / the version of you the system rejects is the version that saves it). The kid laughs; the adult cries. Both consented to the same hour.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (the engine):**
- **The Vivid World.** Production design is character. The world has a specific visual grammar (Spider-Verse's halftone-comic, Wreck-It Ralph's game-engine layers, Encanto's living house, Berk's Viking-meets-dragon ecology). Frame-one, you know where you are.
- **The Misfit's Specific Gift.** The Hero has a non-conforming competence (drawing, listening to fish, talking to ghosts, building junk into rockets, befriending the impossible). This gift is what the world dismisses, then needs.
- **The Impossible Friend.** The Sidekick is often an impossible-tech or impossible-creature companion (Toothless, BMO, the spider-pig, the inheritance ghost). Toyetic and merchandisable. The kid's favorite.
- **The Single Clean Goal.** A clear external destination — return the heart of Tefiti, win the race, save Christmas, find the bonded soul. The kid can name the goal at minute 15.
- **The Adult Theorem.** Under the surface plot, ONE emotional argument is being proven for the parent audience (your family is real even when it's not blood / loss is the price of love / the version of you the world rejects is the version it needs). The kid feels it; the adult names it on the drive home.

**Supporting tropes (stack 5-10 per work):**
- **The Joke Density.** A laugh per ~45 seconds — visual gag, character beat, smart wordplay; the laugh is the kid's ticket to staying engaged for the adult-payload moments.
- **The Big-Number Setpiece.** A signature large-scale sequence (the first dragon flight, the dimensional jump, the dance number, the lava-creature climax). Trailer-bait; the moment kids re-enact.
- **The Parental Wound Reveal.** A scene where the Parental Figure's own wound is shown (Coco's Mama Imelda, Up's Carl's loss, How To Train Your Dragon's Stoick's bias). The reveal lands the adult-theorem.
- **The Cry Moment.** ONE scene engineered for the adult audience to cry. Pixar's specialty; DreamWorks does it lighter; both genres require it. (Up's opening; Coco's "Remember Me"; HTTYD's "Stoick sees Toothless.")
- **The Song / Anthem.** A musical number that compresses theme into hook (Moana's "How Far I'll Go," Encanto's "Surface Pressure," Lego Movie's "Everything Is Awesome"). Not required for non-musical entries but expected.
- **The Sidekick's Sacrifice / Wound.** The Impossible Friend is hurt, lost, or risks itself near the climax. The Hero's grief unlocks the resolution.
- **The Wrong Lesson Reversed.** Mid-film, the Hero thinks the moral is X (conform, give up the gift, accept the parent's wisdom). The climax proves the inverse — and the parent must learn too.
- **The Vivid Background Cast.** 6-12 named ambient characters in the world — village, family, classroom, crew. The world feels populated.
- **The Reconciliation Beat.** Late in act three, the Parental Figure SEES the Hero — sometimes in dialogue, sometimes in action (Maui salutes Moana, Stoick rides Toothless, Mama Coco remembers). The kid's emotional reward.
- **The Self-Aware Moment.** A meta-beat acknowledging the genre — characters know they're in a story (Lego Movie, The Bad Guys), or the framing collapses (Spider-Verse). Optional but increasingly canonical.
- **The Found Family Coda.** Final scene shows the Hero's chosen group — sometimes blood, sometimes not, always assembled-by-love. Final image.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't have the Hero conform to win. The Hero's gift is the gift; suppressing it is the Wrong Lesson.
- Don't have the adult-theorem be cynical. "Adults don't care" / "everything is meaningless" violates four-quadrant. Adult-theorems are bittersweet, not bitter.
- Don't kill the Sidekick permanently. Wounds, near-deaths, brief losses are canonical; permanent death of the kid's favorite character violates the contract.
- Don't end without the Parental Figure either seeing the Hero OR being meaningfully mourned. Unresolved parental wounds are a different genre.
- Don't make the Antagonist a misunderstood good guy who just needs a hug at scale. Some antagonists do soften (Hans is rare-late twist; Tamatoa stays bad) — but the system itself must change.
- Don't preach the theorem. Show the theorem in action; let the audience name it. Voice-over morals violate the four-quadrant contract.
- Don't sacrifice production-design specificity for budget. The Vivid World is non-negotiable; visual world-building is the genre's currency.
- Don't have the kid-tier humor be lazy (potty jokes, slapstick without setup). Joke density must hold up — Lord/Miller-school wit, not lowest-common-denominator.

## Conformity rules
- The Vivid World is non-negotiable. Production-design specificity drives the genre — the world must be drawable from memory after one episode.
- Joke density must hold at roughly one laugh per ~45 seconds. The humor is the kid's ticket to staying engaged through the emotional payloads.
- The Adult Theorem must be specific, namable, and proven through action (not voice-over). One theorem per work — overstuffed themes break the four-quadrant.
- The Misfit's specific gift, established in Episode 1-3, must resolve the climax. No power-up to a new ability the world hasn't seen. The Hero wins by being themselves at full extension.
- The Sidekick must survive. Wounds, scares, near-death — yes. Permanent death of the kid's favorite character violates the contract.
- The Cry Moment must be present. One scene engineered for adult catharsis. Pixar-tier specificity preferred; DreamWorks-tier sentimentality acceptable.
- The Parental Figure must either See the Hero or be meaningfully mourned by the end. Unresolved parental dismissal undercuts the genre's emotional argument.
- The world's system, not just the Antagonist, must visibly change. Hero changes the village/family/multiverse, not just defeats one bad guy.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- World aesthetic — Viking-fantasy (HTTYD), magical-realism-Latin-America (Coco, Encanto), Polynesian-mythic (Moana), comic-book-multiverse (Spider-Verse), video-game-meta (Wreck-It Ralph, Lego Movie), domestic-suburban (Mitchells), fairy-tale-revisionist (Puss in Boots, Klaus), cosmic-emotional (Inside Out, Up). All canonical.
- The Antagonist's nature — embodied villain (Tamatoa, Hans, the witch in Klaus), system-as-antagonist (Berk's law, the Spider-Verse canon, the family curse), self-as-antagonist (Inside Out's denial). Pick the texture; mix is acceptable.
- Musical or non-musical — Disney mainline is musical; Pixar mainline is non-musical; both work. The Song / Anthem is optional.
- Tonal register — broad-comic (Lego, Bad Guys, Mitchells) through earnest-warm (HTTYD, Moana, Encanto) to prestige-grief (Up, Inside Out, Coco). The dial is joke-density vs grief-density.
- The Hero's specific gift — drawing (HTTYD's Hiccup), music (Coco's Miguel), engineering (Mitchells' Katie), empathy (Inside Out's Riley via Joy), Polynesian navigation (Moana). The gift is always non-violent and specific.
- Whether the Antagonist is softened or remains opposed — Frozen-school (Hans stays bad), Wreck-It Ralph-school (King Candy reveals as villain), Pixar-school (often the antagonist is misread until late). Pick a model; commit.
- Cultural specificity — Disney's pivot toward culturally-grounded worlds (Coco, Moana, Encanto) is the modern standard. Generic-fantasy worlds without cultural specificity feel dated.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the Wrong Lesson must cost the Hero the friend, the Cry Moment must consume the parental wound, and the climax must run on the early gift plus the world's planted route) — the content of every edge varies freely by world, theorem, and antagonist texture.

## Caveats
- The genre is the most engineered slot in the corpus. Every beat is workshopped against test audiences. The recipe reflects what passes; deviating breaks the four-quadrant.
- The serialized variants (Avatar, Trollhunters, Spider-Verse animated series, Encanto's coming series) compress the spine into mini-arcs but keep the engine. Multi-season works extend the Adult Theorem across cycles.
- Common failure mode: the Adult Theorem reads too dark for kids OR too light for adults. The four-quadrant requires both audiences leaving satisfied. Test: would a six-year-old laugh in the middle? Would a thirty-year-old cry at the end? If either is no, the work is in a different slot.
- Common failure mode: the Vivid World is generic. Production design must be specific and culturally / aesthetically grounded — "fantasy kingdom" without a real-world or strongly-imagined cultural foundation feels flat.
- Common failure mode: the Cry Moment overplaying. Pixar-tier restraint outperforms DreamWorks-tier sentiment in the long run; aim for the specific gesture, not the swelling music alone.
- Common failure mode: the Hero's gift becoming generic agency at the climax. The specific gift established early must do the final work. No new powers.
- Project-specific note: the recipe is FLOOR. Persona-driven polemic colors the Vivid World (which culture, which aesthetic, which system), the Adult Theorem (what specific argument is proven), and the Antagonist's nature. But the recipe's core — Misfit Hero + specific gift + Impossible Friend + single clean goal + adult catharsis through kid-surface — cannot be removed without leaving the slot.
