# Talent Show Stage

## Slot
A live performance is the climax; rehearsal is the body of the work; the protagonist's hidden self emerges on stage. Music, dance, drag, drumming, comedy — the discipline varies, but the catharsis is the same: the performer steps under the lights and either reveals what they couldn't say or destroys themselves trying. The stage is the engine of truth.

## Emotional contract
**Performance + stakes + reveal.** The audience falls in love with someone whose gift is buried under doubt, family, scarcity, or self-destruction; watches rehearsal extract real cost (the body, the relationship, the sanity); and pays off on stage where the performer either becomes themselves fully — or loses themselves doing it. The cocktail is rehearsal-as-purgatory + stage-as-revelation. The catharsis is the moment the lights come up and the truth has nowhere to hide.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (the engine):**
- **The Hidden Gift.** The Performer's actual ability is shown in a private moment in act one — alone in a room, singing in the car, dancing in front of a mirror, playing in the basement. The audience sees what the world doesn't yet.
- **The Rehearsal Sequence.** Repeated rehearsal scenes — same routine, slight variations, body and craft visibly improving. The film's body is here; the stage is only the climax.
- **The Mentor's Method.** The Mentor's specific approach to the craft — Fletcher's tempo abuse, the ballet master's discipline, Vince Lombardi-style coaching, the patient ear (Once). The Method IS the philosophy.
- **The Stage.** The climactic performance venue — the showcase, the gala, the open mic, the Carnegie, the talent show, the live broadcast. Lit, framed, ceremonial.
- **The Reveal Performance.** At the climax, the Performer takes the stage and either reveals what they've been hiding OR destroys themselves trying. Truth-under-lights.

**Supporting tropes (stack 5-10 per work):**
- **The First Performance.** Early in act one, the Performer performs in a small venue — open mic, school assembly, audition, bar, drag club, family living room. Sets the floor.
- **The Bad Performance.** A failed performance — choking, missing the note, drying onstage, getting booed. The Setback's voice.
- **The Practice Room / Studio.** The specific space where the work happens — piano room, ballet bar, drum kit basement, drag house, garage band space. Lived-in, character.
- **The Number / Set Piece.** A signature performance moment with full production value — the musical number, the solo, the lift, the riff. Trailer-bait; the moment the audience replays.
- **The Mentor's Demand.** A specific Mentor demand the Performer must meet — be the next great drummer, lose ten pounds, drop the accent, kill the safe version of yourself. The bar set.
- **The Cost on the Body.** Physical toll — bleeding hands (Whiplash), broken toes (Black Swan), exhaustion (Vox Lux), addiction (Star Is Born). Body is the genre's grammar.
- **The Industry Room.** A meeting with label, producer, casting director, network — the System's face revealed. Often demoralizing.
- **The Backstage Crisis.** Minutes before the curtain — the missed costume, the broken instrument, the Partner not arrived, the panic attack. Tension cranks.
- **The Hidden Crowd.** The Performer learns who is in the audience — the estranged parent, the Mentor, the Rival, the ex. Stakes elevate.
- **The Standing Ovation (or Its Refusal).** The audience-within either roars or doesn't. The verdict.
- **The Empty Stage.** Post-performance scene — the venue empty, the lights down, the Performer alone with what just happened. Reflection beat.
- **The Cover or the Original.** Whether the Performer covers a known song / role / drag-style or performs an original — choice carries meaning (Once goes original; Pitch Perfect mostly cover; Star Is Born does original).

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't have the Performer succeed without rehearsal cost. Effortless rise reads cheap; the body / sanity / relationship must pay.
- Don't have the Mentor be uncomplicated. Demanding mentors must be morally complex (Fletcher's brutality has truth in it; the ballet master's discipline has cost). Pure villain Mentors flatten.
- Don't resolve the System cleanly. The industry, the family, the scarcity — the System persists; the Performer changed within it, not destroyed it.
- Don't have the Reveal Performance fail to be a reveal. The climactic performance must show the audience-in-the-theater something they didn't know — about the Performer, the craft, or the truth.
- Don't end on triumph without aftermath. Even successful Reveal Performances need the Empty Stage beat. The genre's catharsis is bittersweet.
- Don't make the Rival's defeat the central catharsis. The Performer's relationship to their own gift, not victory over the Rival, is the engine.
- Don't have the Partner be only supportive. The Partner must have stakes — eclipse, jealousy, sacrifice, departure. Pure cheerleader Partners flatten.
- Don't explain the gift through inspirational dialogue. The gift is shown in performance; talk about it kills it.
- Don't sanitize the body cost. Bleeding fingers, twisted ankles, addiction, eating disorders, breakdowns — the genre requires the visible toll. Clean victories read fake.

## Conformity rules
- The Hidden Gift must be shown in a private moment before any public performance. The audience must see what the world doesn't yet.
- Rehearsal is the body of the work, not the stage. Rehearsal Sequences and Practice Room scenes must outweigh stage scenes by ratio. Talent shows that skip rehearsal read fake.
- The Cost on the Body must be visible. Bleeding, exhaustion, addiction, eating, breakdown — the price of the gift must be physical, not just emotional.
- The Reveal Performance must reveal something. New truth about the Performer, the craft, or the relationship between them. Surface-only climactic performances break the contract.
- The Stage is non-negotiable. A formal performance venue (theater, club, broadcast, gala, contest, drag ball) frames the climax. Diffuse climaxes break the engine.
- The Mentor must be morally complex. Pure-villain Mentors flatten; pure-saint Mentors flatten. The Method has truth and cost in it.
- The aftermath beat (Empty Stage, post-performance quiet) is mandatory. Even successful Reveals require the reflection beat to seal the catharsis.
- The System persists. The industry, the family, the scarcity continues after the climax; the Performer changed inside it, not abolished it.
- The Reveal Performance must consume a specific truth withheld in the First Performance and a version rebuilt in the Return to the Practice Room; scale without transformed material is not revelation.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Discipline — singing (Star Is Born, Sing Street, Once, Pitch Perfect, Tick Tick Boom, Vox Lux), drumming (Whiplash), ballet (Black Swan), Broadway / theater (Birdman, Smash, Tick Tick Boom), drag (Pose, RuPaul), choral / a cappella (Pitch Perfect, Glee), musical-theater-school (Fame, Glee), circus (Greatest Showman). Each generates different body / craft texture.
- Tonal register — earnest-musical (Once, Sing Street, Pitch Perfect), anthemic-bittersweet (Star Is Born, La La Land, Tick Tick Boom), obsessive-destructive (Whiplash, Black Swan, Vox Lux), community-celebratory (Pose, Glee at its best). The dial is joyful-craft vs soul-cost.
- The System's nature — industry (label, studio, Broadway), family (immigrant parent, sibling rivalry, religious family), scarcity (poverty, day job, the body's age, immigration status), institution (conservatory, school, military band). Pick the specific pressure.
- The Mentor's specific method — brutal (Whiplash's Fletcher, Black Swan's master), patient-elder (Once's father figure, Tick Tick Boom's Sondheim), drag-mother (Pose's Blanca, Elektra), commercial-coach (Greatest Showman's Barnum). All canonical.
- Solo or ensemble — solo Performer (Star Is Born, Whiplash, Black Swan, Birdman) vs ensemble (Pose, Glee, Pitch Perfect, Fame, Smash). Solo concentrates the self-destruction risk; ensemble distributes it.
- The climactic outcome — triumphant (Pitch Perfect, Sing Street's finale, Tick Tick Boom's "Sunday"), bittersweet (Star Is Born, La La Land), destructive (Black Swan, Vox Lux's complicated stardom), ambiguous (Whiplash's final solo). All canonical.
- Live or competition format — single climactic performance (Star Is Born, Black Swan, Whiplash) vs competition-arc (Pitch Perfect, Pose's balls, RuPaul's runway). Competition adds Rivals as structural feature.
- Whether the Performer is paying for the gift with their life — Star Is Born's tradition (the older partner pays the cost so the younger can rise), Vox Lux (the body of the star sacrificed), Black Swan (literal). Used carefully, this is the genre's prestige register.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the First Performance withholds the truth; rehearsal creates craft and bodily debt; the Bad Performance consumes that debt; the Reveal consumes the rebuilt number) — every discipline, wound, demand, and verdict varies freely.

## Caveats
- The genre rewards specificity. Generic "follow your dreams" musicals without specific cost-on-the-body read soft. Audiences expect the bleeding hands.
- Common failure mode: skipping rehearsal cost. Time-jumping from amateur to virtuoso without showing the body's price breaks the contract. (Now encoded in the wiring: E3's dangerous demand creates E4-body-wound, which constrains E6-10.)
- Common failure mode: the Reveal Performance fails to reveal. Big production numbers without new emotional truth read as music videos, not climaxes. (Now encoded in the wiring: E2-safe-cover, E8-reveal-arrangement, E10.)
- Common failure mode: the Mentor as villain or saint. The Method must be morally complex — even Fletcher has truth; even Sondheim has limits. (Now encoded in the wiring: E3's Method has craft truth and known danger; E8 forces its verdict.)
- Common failure mode: the Partner reduced to supportive love interest. The Partner's eclipse / rivalry / sacrifice / departure is the genre's secondary emotional engine. (Now encoded in the wiring: E6-partner-absence constrains E7, E9, and E10.)
- Common failure mode: the System reformed. The genre's adult truth is that the industry / family / scarcity persists. Pure-uplift endings undercut the bittersweet. (Now encoded in E5's gatekeeper term and E10's persistent System.)
- The genre overlaps with sports underdog (training cost, structured climax), with biopic (when based on real performer), with romance (Partner subplots). Distinguish: talent show stage runs on Hidden Gift + rehearsal cost + Reveal Performance under lights. Strip the live-performance climax and it's a different recipe.
- Drag and reality-competition variants (Pose, RuPaul's Drag Race) deserve special handling — the runway / ball IS the recurring stage, and the season's climax is often a final Grand Performance rather than a single Reveal. The recipe holds; the unit of Stage is the ball-arc, not the season-arc.
- The serialized variant (Glee, Pose, Smash, Fame) compresses the spine into per-episode mini-Reveals + season-arc large Reveal. The Practice Room / Rehearsal scenes are weekly anchors.
- Project-specific note: the recipe is FLOOR. Persona-driven polemic colors the System (industry, family, scarcity), the discipline (which craft), the Mentor's specific method, the Performer's specific cost. But the recipe's core — Hidden Gift + visible rehearsal cost + Reveal Performance under lights — cannot be removed. (Now encoded in the wiring from E1-private-original through E10.)
