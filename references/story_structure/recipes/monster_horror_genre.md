# Monster Horror (Beast + Isolation + Survival)

## Slot
Serialized horror where a clearly-defined, embodied creature isolates a small group of people in a constrained location and hunts them. The monster has rules — a fixed grammar of what triggers it, what hurts it, what it wants. The show is the cat-and-mouse, the chamber, and what the monster's grammar reveals about the people forced to play by it. The audience pays in tension that has a body.

## Corpus
- *A Quiet Place* (Krasinski)
- *Jaws* (Spielberg)
- *Tremors* (Underwood)
- *The Mist* (Darabont)
- *Bird Box* (Bier)
- *30 Days of Night* (Slade)
- *Predator* (McTiernan)
- *The Descent* (Marshall)
- *The Ritual* (Bruckner)
- *Bone Tomahawk* (Zahler)
- *The Thing* (Carpenter, 1982)
- *Trollhunter* (Øvredal)
- *Cloverfield* (Reeves)
- *Crawl* (Aja)
- *Resident Evil* (franchise)

(Tonal range: Tremors' creature-feature pulp and Trollhunter's mockumentary-genre through A Quiet Place's family-prestige to The Descent's existential-survival and The Thing's paranoid-prestige. Same recipe — named creature, constrained chamber, group hunted, monster's rules reveal the people.)

## Emotional contract
**Tension + chase + reveal.** The audience knows there is a monster, knows roughly what it can do, and watches a small group either learn its rules in time or die discovering them. The cocktail is breath-held suspense interrupted by violent payoff and ended with one or two survivors who know what the monster cost. Catharsis is survival, but survival paid for in named losses.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Monster Has Rules** — A named, learnable grammar — triggered by sound (A Quiet Place), light (30 Days), motion (Tremors), eye contact (Bird Box), scent, heartbeat, water. The audience LEARNS the rules across the show and watches characters break them with named consequences.
- **The Chamber** — A constrained location the group cannot leave — boat, cave, town in storm, farmhouse, mall, snowed-in lodge, Arctic station, flooded house. The chamber's geography is mapped early and exploited late.
- **The Signature Kill** — The monster's first kill must be visually distinctive — bisection, suction, snatch, infection, freeze. The kill teaches the audience the threat in one moment. Every subsequent kill rhymes.
- **The Read** — Mid-season, a character figures out a specific rule that changes how the group operates. The Read is the show's mechanical engine — each Read raises survival odds and lowers them in a new way.
- **The Final Confrontation in the Monster's Terms** — Climax happens on the monster's home ground or in conditions that favor the monster. The protagonist wins by USING the rules, not by overpowering. Skill over force.

**Supporting tropes:**
- **The First Detection** — Animals behave wrong, instruments spike, the experienced local goes quiet. The monster's presence registered before its appearance.
- **The Disbelieved Witness** — Someone saw it early; they were dismissed. They die first or are the Expert by Episode 3.
- **The Stupid Decision** — One character ignores a rule (radio on, light on, foot in the water) for a defensible emotional reason (grief, love, fear). The audience screams; the monster arrives. The genre requires at least two of these.
- **The Lockdown Inventory** — A scene where the group counts what they have — ammo, food, water, batteries, days. Tension architecture; the audience starts a clock.
- **The Wrong-Headed Plan** — The Skeptic or an outside authority insists on a plan that uses the wrong rules. Following the plan gets people killed. The protagonist must break with the plan.
- **The Map Reveal** — Mid-season, the Chamber's geography is mapped — vents, tunnels, blind spots, choke points. The geography becomes character.
- **The False Safe Zone** — A location the group thinks is safe (cellar, attic, boat, bunker, church). It isn't. Discovered as a kill set-piece.
- **The Monster's Behavior Pattern** — The monster does the same thing on a schedule (feeds at night, hunts in fog, returns to a den, vocalizes before strike). The protagonist times their move to it.
- **The Sacrificial Stand** — A character holds off the monster so the others can move. They die, knowingly, often with a final line. Marks the midpoint loss.
- **The One Bullet Left** — Late-stage scarcity made physical — last round, last fuel, last battery, last hour of light. Resource depletion as suspense.
- **The Survivor's Headcount** — End-of-season tally. Who walks out, who doesn't, what they carry. The genre's moral accounting.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't keep the monster vague. The audience must know roughly what it is, what it does, and what its rules are by Episode 3. Mystery is for cosmic horror; monster horror is mechanical.
- Don't make the monster supernatural-without-rules. Even if it's a ghost or a god, it must have a learnable grammar. Bird Box: don't look. The Mist: don't go out. A Quiet Place: don't make sound.
- Don't let the protagonist win by overpowering. They win by reading the rules and using them. Brute force is the wrong genre (action).
- Don't kill the Innocent / Stake casually. Their loss must be either the season's climactic gut-punch or they survive. No middle.
- Don't have the Outside World rescue the protagonists. Help either doesn't come, comes too late, or arrives compromised. The chamber is the show.
- Don't let the Skeptic be right. Their denial must cost — usually a body — before they convert. The genre is the cost of refusing to believe.
- Don't reveal the monster fully in Episode 1. Reveal in pieces — claw, shadow, sound, partial silhouette — full body somewhere between Ep 3 and Ep 5.

## Conformity rules
- The monster must have a NAMED, LEARNABLE grammar by Episode 3. Mystery monsters belong to cosmic horror.
- The Chamber must be mapped — the audience must understand the geography by Episode 4.
- The protagonist must win by READING the monster, not by overpowering it. Force is the wrong genre.
- The Signature Kill must happen in Episode 1, with full violence and partial monster reveal. The threat level is established immediately.
- The Sacrificial Brave must die mid-season, knowingly. The midpoint cost is non-negotiable.
- The Innocent / Stake must be specifically vulnerable to the monster's grammar — deafness for sound, blindness for sight, etc. The vulnerability is the show's tension architecture.
- At least one character must survive. Monster horror is not nihilist; survival is the catharsis. The headcount is the moral accounting.
- The Outside World must fail to rescue — too late, compromised, or absent. The chamber is the show.

- Failure mode: keeping the monster vague past Episode 3. Audiences lose patience; mechanical tension requires mechanics. If the monster is meant to be ambiguous, the genre is cosmic horror — different recipe.
- Failure mode: winning by overwhelming firepower. Monster horror is skill-based; force is action. If the climax is a shootout, the chamber's rules didn't matter and the show didn't either.
- Failure mode: the Skeptic as cartoon. Their denial must be defensible — institutional, parental, financial. If they're just stupid, the cost of their conversion doesn't land.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Monster type — biological (Jaws, Crawl, Tremors), supernatural-with-rules (Bird Box, A Quiet Place creatures), infectious (The Thing, Resident Evil), human-class predator (30 Days vampires, Predator). Each shifts the kill grammar.
- The monster's signature sense — sound, sight, scent, heat, motion, taste, vibration. Determines what the group must suppress.
- Chamber type — boat, cave, town, farm, mall, station, plane, island, house. Each has its own choke points.
- Group size — minimal (A Quiet Place: 4) to ensemble (The Mist, The Thing). Smaller = more interiority; larger = more headcount.
- Tonal register — pulp creature-feature, family-prestige, existential-survival, mockumentary, paranoid-prestige. Recipe is constant.
- Whether the monster is a known species (shark, alligator, troll) or invented. Known species sharpens visceral fear; invented allows weirder grammar.
- Era — contemporary, period (30 Days frontier-feel), post-apocalyptic, near-future. Era is a register dial.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (detection proves the trigger; denial chooses the refuge and failed rescue; the map, feeding clock, and paid lure become the final weapon) — every monster, stimulus, chamber, trail, and trap varies freely.

## Caveats
- Register dial: pulp accelerates the First Detection into a cold open and full monster reveal by Ep 2; prestige hides the monster until Ep 5. Both work; the beat spine survives.
- Genre proximity: cosmic horror has no learnable rules; folk horror has a place that's hunting; monster horror has a beast you can hide from. The line is whether the antagonist has a body and a grammar.
- Resident Evil and The Thing extend monster horror into "infection" subgenre — the monster's grammar is conversion, the Expert's role expands. Same recipe; the monster's grammar dial is just turned to "paranoia."
