# Folk Horror (Land + Ritual + Ancestral Curse)

## Slot
Serialized horror where an outsider (or a returning native who has been gone long enough to count as one) arrives at a specific PLACE — village, farm, valley, island, family estate — that holds a buried covenant with something older. The land itself remembers; the locals are its keepers; the protagonist's blood, grief, or arrival has reopened the contract. Horror through landscape, ritual, and lineage rather than monster.

## Corpus
- *The Witch* (Eggers)
- *Midsommar* (Aster)
- *The Wicker Man* (1973, Hardy)
- *Hereditary* (Aster)
- *Apostle* (Evans)
- *The Ritual* (Bruckner)
- *His House* (Weekes)
- *Lamb* (Jóhannsson)
- *The Pale Blue Eye* (Cooper)
- *Men* (Garland)
- *The Dead Lands* (Hyperion)
- *The Northman* (Eggers)
- *Saint Maud* (Glass)
- *Yellowjackets* (Showtime)
- *Sharp Objects* (HBO / Flynn)

(Tonal range: The Witch's austere-art and Saint Maud's prestige-minimal through Midsommar's bright-folk and His House's quiet-displacement to Apostle's grungy-grindhouse and The Wicker Man's pulp-pagan. Same recipe — land that remembers, ritual that demands, outsider whose blood reopens the contract.)

## Emotional contract
**Inheritance + ritual + reckoning.** The audience watches an outsider learn that the place is conscious, the locals are participants, and the protagonist is not random — the land called them. The cocktail is the slow tightening of a contract that was signed before they were born. Catharsis is either submission or destruction; comfortable escape is not on the menu.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Arrival Under Pretext** — The Outsider comes for a non-horror reason: a funeral, a wedding, a research grant, a writing retreat, a fresh start, a job, a pilgrimage. The pretext must be plausible and personal. The land's pull is invisible to them.
- **The Land Remembers** — The Place is treated as conscious. Weather changes around the protagonist's emotional state. Animals look. Stones recur. The land is the antagonist, expressed environmentally.
- **The Welcoming Ritual** — Early ceremony, meal, dance, song, gift-giving that the Outsider mistakes for hospitality. It is hospitality AND initiation; both true.
- **The Buried Covenant** — A contract made by ancestors with the land / the dead / the Old Ones. Periodic payment required. The protagonist's arrival is the payment coming due.
- **The Liturgical Climax** — Ritual at the end performed at full ceremony — costumes, song, fire, blood. The Outsider is either the offering, the new Elder, or the one who breaks it. Maximum aesthetic commitment.

**Supporting tropes:**
- **The Welcoming Bowl** — A meal, drink, or gift in the first act that the Outsider consumes without thinking. Later revealed as binding.
- **The Folk Song** — A song the locals all know, sung at gatherings, with lyrics that turn out to encode the contract. The Outsider hears it three times before they understand.
- **The Old Photograph** — In the Elder's house, on a wall: a photograph that includes a face that should not be there (or matches the Outsider's). The covenant's record.
- **The Wrong Animal** — Livestock that watches, sheep with a face, a hare that follows, a crow with a name. The Land's interface.
- **The Ritual Garment** — A specific costume — flower crown, animal mask, white dress, painted skin — the Outsider is dressed in for the final ceremony. Aesthetically beautiful, semantically terminal.
- **The Failed Escape** — A first attempt to leave that goes wrong — car breaks down, road floods, phone dies, train cancelled. The Land closing the door.
- **The Local's Confession** — Mid-season, the Local Friend admits a partial truth — "this is what we do here" — without naming what. Test of whether the Outsider can hear it.
- **The Elder's Hospitality** — The Elder is genuinely kind. Tea, room, advice, comfort. Their kindness is not deception; it is doctrine. Belonging IS the offer.
- **The Ancestor's Object** — A photograph, ring, letter, scar, name shared with the protagonist's family. Lineage closes around them.
- **The Dance** — Communal dance scene in which the Outsider either joins (committing) or stands apart (marking themselves for removal). Folk horror's commitment threshold.
- **The Witness Breaks** — The earlier survivor finally tells the protagonist what is happening — usually in a fragmented, terrified, half-mad scene that the protagonist only fully understands in retrospect.
- **The Burning** — Fire as climax — a wicker structure, a barn, an effigy, the protagonist's own house. Folk horror's signature image.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't have the Outsider be a stranger by accident. The land called them — for blood, grief, faith, or unfinished business. Random tourists are the wrong genre.
- Don't make the locals stupid, backward, or comic. They are intelligent, articulate, and certain. Their belief must be coherent enough that the audience partly understands it.
- Don't reveal the contract through monologue. The Outsider must ASSEMBLE it from the folk song, the photograph, the welcoming bowl, the Witness's fragment, the Ancestor's object. Earn the worldbuilding.
- Don't introduce a monster. Folk horror's antagonist is the Place and its keepers. If something appears with claws and a name, it's monster horror.
- Don't let the Outsider escape clean. They either submit (become participant), break the cycle (at unsurvivable cost), or are consumed. Driving away unscathed is the wrong genre.
- Don't set the climax indoors. The Land's ritual happens on the land — fields, woods, cliffs, water. Outside, under sky.
- Don't make the Elder a hypocrite. The Elder must believe. Their conviction is the audience's hardest sit.

## Conformity rules
- The Place must be specific, named, and visually consistent — village, valley, island, farm, estate. The audience must be able to map it.
- The Elder must be sympathetic, articulate, and certain. If they are cartoonish villains the genre collapses into camp.
- The Land must be expressed environmentally — weather, animals, light, soil — not as an entity with dialogue or a face.
- The Outsider was called. Their grief, lineage, faith, or biography is the hook. Random tourists drift into monster horror.
- The contract must be ASSEMBLED across the season, not explained in one scene. Folk Song + photograph + bowl + artifact + witness fragment — the audience earns the worldbuilding.
- The Liturgical Climax must happen outdoors, in full ceremony, with maximum aesthetic commitment.
- The Outsider cannot drive away clean. Submission, sacrifice, or destruction — pick one. Folk horror does not let you leave.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- The Land's signature — Norse fjord, English moor, American Appalachia, African forest, Pacific island, Eastern European village, frontier homestead. Folk horror is regionally specific.
- The covenant's deity / agency — old gods, the dead, the woods, a saint gone wrong, a witch's pact, a colonial ghost. The land's WHO is the register dial.
- Whether the Outsider submits, breaks, or is destroyed. All three are valid endings; choose deliberately.
- Religious framing — pagan, Christian-heretical, syncretic, secular-superstition. Each shifts the ritual's grammar.
- Whether the Local Friend becomes lover, sibling-figure, or guide. Romance is optional; complicity is mandatory.
- Era — historical (The Witch), contemporary (Midsommar), period-mythic (The Northman). Era is a register dial; the recipe is constant.
- Tonal register — austere-art, bright-folk, grungy-grindhouse, prestige-minimal. Folk horror handles all.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the bowl binds; the song, portrait, object, and fragment assemble the covenant; the failed escape becomes the Friend's betrayal; the marked field becomes the climax ground) — every land, payment, artifact, and final answer varies freely.

## Caveats
- Failure mode: the locals as caricature. If the village is "creepy yokels," the genre dies on impact. The Elder must be a person the audience could love.
- Failure mode: explaining the covenant in one scene. Folk horror requires assembly. Dump the worldbuilding and the dread evaporates. (Now encoded in the wiring: E1's bowl, E2's song, E3's portrait and object, and E5's fragment assemble E7.)
- Failure mode: the Outsider as passive victim. They must ACT — investigate, attempt escape, choose. The horror is what the land does to a person who is trying. (Now encoded in the wiring: the Outsider stays, warns, investigates, attempts escape, and makes the final vow.)
- Register dial: austere-art slows the Welcoming Ritual to two episodes of pure pastoral; grindhouse compresses arrival-to-folk-song into a cold open. Both work; the beat spine survives.
- Genre proximity: cosmic horror's antagonist is indifferent; folk horror's land is HUNGRY and PATIENT — it wants something specific, periodically. The line is whether the antagonist has appetites.
- Yellowjackets and Sharp Objects extend folk horror into multi-season serialized — the Land's memory plays out over decades. The recipe scales; the contract just takes longer to surface.
