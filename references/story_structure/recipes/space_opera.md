# Space Opera (Wonder + Peril + Exaltation)

## Slot
Serialized interstellar fiction where a small crew, family, or single rising figure crosses into a galaxy-scale conflict — political, military, mystical, or species-wide — that they did not start and cannot leave. The story is intimate at the deck level and operatic at the systems level. The protagonist's choices ripple through fleets, planets, and centuries. The genre's voltage is the contrast between one cup of coffee on a bridge and the fall of an empire.

## Corpus
- *Star Wars* (Lucas, Original Trilogy + *Andor*)
- *Star Trek* (TOS / TNG / DS9 / *Strange New Worlds*)
- *Dune* (Herbert / Villeneuve)
- *The Expanse* (Corey / Syfy / Amazon)
- *Battlestar Galactica* (Moore)
- *Babylon 5* (Straczynski)
- *Mass Effect* (BioWare)
- *Foundation* (Asimov / Apple)
- *Farscape* (Henson / O'Bannon)
- *Ender's Game* (Card)
- *Guardians of the Galaxy* (Gunn)
- *Killjoys* (Syfy)
- *Firefly* (Whedon)
- *The Mandalorian* (Favreau / Filoni)
- *Hyperion* (Simmons)

(Tonal range: from Star Wars OT's pulp-Lucas mythic and Guardians of the Galaxy's mixtape-camp through Mandalorian and Killjoys' pulp-procedural to Andor and BSG's prestige-political and Dune / Foundation's prestige-philosophical. Same recipe — small lives inside galaxy-scale stakes, with exaltation as the closing emotion.)

## Emotional contract
**Wonder + peril + exaltation.** The audience opens on the size — strange ships, strange skies, strange physics — gets pulled into a peril that is both personal and civilizational, and ends with the protagonist becoming part of something cosmically larger than themselves. Exaltation is not destiny achieved; it is the moment the protagonist accepts their place in the scale. The cocktail is awe with weight.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (genre backbone):**
- **The Provincial Opening** — Story starts on a small place — farm, freighter, backwater station, minor moon, a desert. The audience meets the protagonist before they know the scale. This is the floor; the rise needs the bottom.
- **The Crew on the Deck** — A handful of bodies in a small pressurized space with names and jobs. The show's emotional currency. We see them eat, fight, joke, mourn, share watch.
- **The Wonder Beat** — Each episode has at least one cosmic-scale image — a planetary horizon, a fleet jump, a strange creature, an unexplained signal, a wreck older than the empire. The wonder is the dose.
- **The Stakes Ladder** — Local → regional → planetary → galactic → cosmic. Episodes climb. Each beat raises the scale of the consequence. The recipe's spinal column.
- **The Final Exaltation** — Climax delivers a moment where the protagonist takes their place in something larger than them. Not "I won" but "I am part of this now." Sometimes triumphant, sometimes tragic, always operatic.

**Supporting tropes:**
- **The Mentor's Pass** — Old hand gives the protagonist the symbolic object (sword, sigil, code, name, ship) and dies / vanishes / departs. The pass is the show's permission to grow.
- **The Lost Order** — A faded institution — Jedi, Bene Gesserit, Foundation, Roganov, Rangers, the prior Federation — that the protagonist is in some way reactivating. Past haunts present.
- **The Bridge Crew Scene** — A pressurized command sequence where bodies on a deck make a decision under fire. The genre's procedural beat.
- **The Diplomatic Fail** — Talks at the top break down — treaty, summit, marriage, council — and the protagonist's faction is exposed. The political feed into the military.
- **The Alien Companion** — A non-human (or barely-human) crew member whose biology, ethics, or worldview is different in a way the show takes seriously. Not a costume — a perspective.
- **The Capital Ship Battle** — Fleet engagement rendered as both spectacle and personal — we see the protagonist's small ship inside the larger formation. Operatic vehicle.
- **The Inheritance** — Protagonist learns their lineage matters — bloodline, prophecy, debt, name, navigator-gene. Sometimes confirmed, sometimes weaponized against them.
- **The Sacrifice Play** — A crew member, the Mentor, or the Tether dies committing to the cause. Spent specifically — not random.
- **The Quiet Bridge Coffee** — A scene of stillness on the ship before the next escalation — coffee on the deck, watch shared, a song over the comm, food at the table. The peace that makes the peril matter.
- **The Council of Powers** — A scene where the great players (Empire, Rebels, Houses, AIs, Aliens) appear in the same room or fleet. Audience sees the size all at once.
- **The Homecoming That Doesn't Fit** — Protagonist returns to the Provincial Opening — and they no longer fit it. The cost of scale.
- **The Found Document** — Star chart, log entry, intercepted signal, prophecy, ancient inscription that names the conflict's deeper history. World expanding.
- **The Trickster's Pivot** — Rogue / smuggler / rival makes the unselfish choice at the moment the protagonist needed them most. Han at the Death Star.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't ground the climax in only the personal. A duel that doesn't echo a war is the wrong genre (that's pulp or noir). The fleet must matter when the lightsaber lands.
- Don't reduce the Wonder to mechanics with no awe. The protomolecule, the Force, psychohistory — these can have rules; they can't have wiki-paragraph completeness.
- Don't make the Empire / Imperium uniformly faceless. The Tyrant must be a person — competent, articulate, with a worldview the protagonist could ALMOST agree with.
- Don't kill the protagonist without exaltation. Tragic ending is allowed; meaningless death is not. The Wonder must witness them.
- Don't resolve the politics in one season. The genre's neighborhood is multi-year, multi-fleet stakes. Single-season closure rings small.
- Don't strand the crew dynamic. If the deck stops being warm, the cocktail collapses into military procedural.
- Don't have the protagonist already know the scale at the start. The rise is the recipe.

## Conformity rules
- The protagonist must start small. Provincial Opening is a floor — farm, freighter, post, station. If they're already an admiral at episode 1, the recipe is wrong.
- The Crew must be a found family by midpoint and must feel warm on the deck. Bridge coffee scenes are required. If the deck is cold the genre slides into military-prestige.
- The Wonder must be present and partial — never reduced to mechanics with no awe. The audience must believe the universe is BIGGER than the protagonist can know.
- The Tyrant must be a person with a worldview, not a faceless empire. Articulate, competent, near-plausible. The Empire must have a face.
- The stakes must climb the ladder — local, regional, planetary, galactic, cosmic. At least three rungs must visibly land per season.
- The climax must EXALT — the protagonist accepts their place in something larger. Tragic exaltation is valid (Andor); cost-free exaltation is not.
- The Tether must be named, on-screen, and at risk. Without them the exaltation reads as ambition.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Tonal register — pulp-mythic (Star Wars OT, Mandalorian), mixtape-camp (Guardians of the Galaxy), prestige-political (Andor, BSG, Expanse), prestige-philosophical (Dune, Foundation, Hyperion), procedural-pulp (Firefly, Killjoys, Farscape).
- Faction shape — Empire vs Rebellion (Star Wars), House vs House (Dune), polity vs corporation (Expanse), Federation vs invader (Trek, Mass Effect), surviving fleet vs everyone (BSG).
- The Wonder's substrate — mystical (Force, spice, Foundation), biological (protomolecule, navigator gene, Reapers), political-mathematical (psychohistory), AI / synthetic (Cylons, Geth), ancient-alien (Mass Effect Reapers, Babylon 5 First Ones).
- The Crew's composition — military (Trek, BSG), criminal (Firefly, Guardians), mercenary (Killjoys, Mandalorian), aristocratic (Dune), mixed-civilian (Expanse). Each register dial.
- Whether the protagonist accepts the Wonder (Paul, Luke, Daenerys-as-cautionary) or refuses it (Andor, Foundation's hard-Seldon strain). Both ride the cocktail.
- Whether the climax is triumphant or tragic. Lucas wins; Andor pays; BSG splits. All conform.
- Visual register — Lucas's pulp-mythic clean, Villeneuve's prestige-hush, Gunn's saturated kitsch, BSG's documentary-handheld, Mandalorian's western-frame.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the council break creates the pass, the Tether hit drives the battle, the climax spends what the season paid for) — the CONTENT of every edge varies freely: which home burns, what the Wonder reveals, whose sacrifice opens the breach, what exaltation costs.

## Caveats
- Register dial: how mystical vs how material the Wonder is. Dune leans mystical; Expanse leans material; Trek varies by episode. Both work; choose deliberately.
- Tonal dial: how OPERATIC the climax. Lucas peaks loud; Andor peaks quiet; Foundation peaks structurally. All exalt; not all crescendo.
- Genre proximity: military sci-fi is space opera with the Wonder muted. If the climax is a tactical victory with no cosmic dimension, the recipe has slid to procedural.
- Common failure mode: the personal duel without the fleet. If the protagonist's climactic moment has no civilizational echo, the recipe is hard sci-fi or pulp adventure — not space opera. The lightsaber needs the war.
- Common failure mode: Wonder as exposition. If a character monologues the metaphysics, the awe collapses. The Wonder is shown — through behavior, image, physics, witness. Dune's spice does, Star Wars' Force does, the protomolecule does.
- Common failure mode: a cold deck. If the Crew never shares quiet — coffee, song, watch, food — the audience can't read the warmth that makes the loss matter.
- Common failure mode: the Tyrant as cartoon. Vader works because of the prequels' Anakin; Dukat works because of his charm; Cain works because she's almost right. The Imperial Face must be ALMOST credible.
