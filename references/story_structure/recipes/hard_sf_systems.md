# Hard SF Systems

## Slot
Plausible-technology science fiction where physics has teeth, characters are competent, and the central puzzle resolves through rigor (engineering, science, deduction, institutional process) rather than wonder, prophecy, or destiny. The catharsis is the reveal landing as RIGOR — a calculation that holds, a system understood, a truth confirmed by evidence. Wonder is permitted, but only after the math.

## Emotional contract
**Physics + collaboration + revelation.** The audience feels the weight of real constraint (orbital mechanics, language, computation, distance, time), watches smart people work the problem together under pressure, and pays off with a reveal that is RIGOROUS — the math holds, the system is understood, the alien is communicated with. The cocktail is competence + constraint + cathartic understanding.

## Trope vocabulary

**Core (the engine):**
- **The Constraint Is Real.** The story is structured around an actual physical or computational constraint — orbital mechanics, light-speed lag, fuel budgets, sample-size requirements, the language barrier. The constraint is named, measured, and binding.
- **The Workroom Scene.** Multiple specialists working a problem together — whiteboards, terminals, scratch paper, simulators. Conversation IS competence. The audience watches the model get built.
- **The Calculation On Screen.** Math, code, diagrams, or domain-specific reasoning is shown — sometimes literally (Apollo 13 slide rule), sometimes through dialogue that reads as math. The genre's grammar.
- **The Rigorous Reveal.** The climax confirms a model or a hypothesis through evidence. The Operator gets it right because they did the work. No deus ex machina, no last-second insight from nowhere.
- **The Cost of Knowing.** Knowing the truth costs something — Louise's daughter (Arrival), the Foundation's burden, the Devs' isolation, Andor's friend. Knowledge has a price.

**Supporting tropes (stack 5-10 per work):**
- **The Procedural Brief.** A short scene where mission objectives are stated formally — flight plan, project charter, mission profile. Specifies what success means.
- **The Failure Mode.** Something goes wrong in a way that matches real failure-modes (vacuum exposure, oxygen depletion, software fault, transmission lag). The audience learns the system by watching it break.
- **The Improvisation.** Smart people MacGyver a solution within real constraints. Duct tape, code patches, orbital improvisation. The catharsis is competence under pressure.
- **The Communication Lag.** Distance / time / language creates real delay. The audience feels the lag in their seat.
- **The Briefing-to-the-Public.** A press conference, a public statement, a televised broadcast — the institution faces the Witnesses. Pressure beat.
- **The Quiet Tech.** Long shots of working tech — gauges, terminals, hardware. The world is real because the hardware is real.
- **The Disciplinary Clash.** Specialist + Operator argue across their domains; both are right within their frames; the synthesis is the puzzle's solution.
- **The Whiteboard Scene.** Someone draws the problem. Sometimes literally on glass. The audience sees the model become legible.
- **The Sacrifice Calculus.** A choice with a math behind it — who gets the oxygen, which signal we trust, which mission we abort. The genre refuses easy moral framing.
- **The Recovery / Return.** After the reveal, return-to-base scenes — the human cost shown, the institution processing the result, debrief.
- **The Long Take of Space (or the Equivalent).** A held shot of the system at scale — Earth from orbit, the simulation visualizing, the seed grown. The audience feels the system's reality.

**Anti-tropes (the genre does NOT do these):**
- Don't have the Operator win by intuition alone. Intuition can spark the question, but the answer must be derived. Rigor closes the deal.
- Don't violate the established physics. If the show says light-speed lag, the lag holds in the climax. If the show says zero-G, no convenient gravity for the fight.
- Don't have the Antagonist Faction be cartoonishly evil. The Faction has institutional logic — budget, mission priority, ideological commitment that reads as real bureaucracy.
- Don't resolve through a single chosen-one with a unique gift. Hard SF resolves through TEAMS — even the Operator's competence is paired with the Specialist, supervised by the Director, supported by the team.
- Don't reveal the puzzle's answer through monologue. The answer must be SHOWN — the simulation runs, the artifact behaves, the equation holds.
- Don't introduce magic or undefined wonder in the climax. New rules can appear, but the new rules must be consistent with the established ones — extension, not contradiction.
- Don't have characters explain the science to each other in expert-to-expert dialogue. Expertise is shown through how they work, not through educational dialogue dumps.
- Don't end on full optimism without cost. The Cost of Knowing must register — even successful missions extract a price.

## Conformity rules
- The constraint must be real. A specific physical / computational / linguistic / institutional limit is named, measured, and binding through the climax.
- The team must be the engine. The Operator does not solo the resolution — the Specialist, the Director, the Theorist all contribute. Lone-genius resolutions are a different recipe.
- The Calculation must appear on screen. Whiteboards, terminals, paper, dialogue that reads as math — the audience watches the model get built.
- The puzzle must resolve through rigor, not revelation-from-elsewhere. New evidence, yes; deus ex machina, no.
- Physics / system rules established in episode 1 must hold in episode 10. Established constraints are the audience's contract.
- The Faction (if used) must have institutional logic, not malice. The audience must understand why the institution defends itself.
- The Cost of Knowing must register in the ending. Even successful missions extract a visible price — a relationship, a colleague, the Operator's certainty about the world.
- Expertise is shown through work, not exposition. Characters do not lecture each other on what they both already know.

## Wiring tests
- Delete-test: no episode can be removed without a later episode breaking.
- The climax consumes at least two character choices from distinct earlier episodes.
- Every named flaw fires as a choice whose consequence is visible in a later episode.
- Every relationship the cast table says moves shows its early state AND its changed
  state as on-screen actions.
- Every cost charged is paid: a later episode is harder in a named way because of it.

## Variation rules
- Scale of the puzzle — single mission (The Martian, Apollo 13), corporate-conspiracy (Devs, Severance), interplanetary politics (Expanse, FAM), first-contact (Arrival, Contact, 3 Body), generational (Foundation, Children of Time, Silo). All canonical.
- Tonal register — optimistic-collaborative (FAM, Martian, Apollo 13), procedural-bleak (Silo, Devs, Severance), austere-cosmic (Arrival, 2001), institutional-grit (Andor, Expanse). The dial is hope-vs-dread.
- The Antagonist's nature — Force (physics, time, alien incomprehension), Faction (corporate, governmental, ideological), Internal (the team's own institution turns), Cosmic (the universe's indifference). Pick the frame.
- Time scale — hours (Apollo 13, Martian crises), months (Martian whole), years (FAM, Expanse), centuries (Foundation, Children of Time), eternal (Devs, 2001). Affects pacing and what "cost" means.
- The Operator's domain — astronaut (Martian, Apollo 13, FAM), engineer (Expanse, Silo), linguist (Arrival), physicist (3 Body, Devs), programmer (Devs, Severance), captain (Expanse), intelligence officer (Andor). Each generates different problem-textures.
- Whether the reveal is comforting or disquieting — Martian (yes we get him home, but at cost) vs Arrival (yes we communicate, but you knew your daughter would die) vs Devs (yes determinism is real, and that is terrifying). All canonical.
- Cultural framing — Cold-War legacy (Apollo, FAM), corporate-future (Severance, Devs), post-collapse (Silo, Expanse), first-contact-with-strangers (Arrival, Contact, 3 Body). The institutional vocabulary varies.
- The wiring's edges are fixed (the First Failure consumes a binned signal; the Faction's leverage is a lie the Director chose; the Reveal consumes the restored reading, the staked denial, and the granted release) — the CONTENT of every edge varies freely: which signal gets binned, what the model's author concealed, what the patch costs, whose life sits inside the math.

## Caveats
- The genre is unusual in tolerating both very optimistic and very bleak registers without leaving the slot. The For All Mankind / Silo distance is enormous — what unites them is rigor.
- Constraint-drift — naming the rule, then ignoring it when convenient — is what hard-SF audiences police most aggressively. (Now encoded in the wiring: E1-physics-rule is consumed by E6 and E10; the climax obeys the opening's rule.)
- Lone-genius drift breaks the slot toward space-opera, and reveal-by-dialogue deflates it. (Both now encoded: E10 cannot run without E7-united-frame — three contributors fused — and must consume E9-one-shot-proof: the evidence runs, it is not recounted.)
- Institutional Antagonists rendered as cartoon villains read cheap to this genre's adult audience. The face needs real bureaucratic logic AND the human wound. (The wound is now encoded in the cast table's Causal obligation: wounded authorship, suppression over admission.)
- Undercooked Cost of Knowing reads thin. (Now encoded: the burned prototype, the staked life, the sanitized report, and the burned career each make a named later episode harder.)
- The genre overlaps with mystery (puzzle-driven), with bureaucratic thriller (institutional pressure), with first-contact SF (alien revelation). Distinguish by the engine: hard SF runs on physics + collaboration + rigorous reveal. Strip the rigor and it's a different genre.
- Project-specific note: the recipe is FLOOR. Persona-driven polemic colors the institutional politics, the kind of cost extracted, the cultural framing of the team. But the recipe's core — real constraint + collaborative team + rigorous evidenced reveal — cannot be removed.
